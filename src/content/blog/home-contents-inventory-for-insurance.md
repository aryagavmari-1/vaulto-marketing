If your home flooded tonight, or you came back from holiday to a break-in, could you tell your insurer exactly what you'd lost — and prove it? Most of us couldn't. We carry a rough sense of what we own, but not a list, not values, and certainly not receipts. That gap is where claims stall, payouts shrink, and stressful weeks get longer.

This guide is about closing that gap *before* anything happens. Not a generic tidy-up list, but an inventory built for one job: **standing up to an insurance claim.** By the end you'll know what to capture for each item so a loss adjuster accepts it, how to match your cover to what you actually own, and how to keep the whole thing somewhere a fire or burglar can't reach.

It's written UK-first, but the principles travel. Contents-cover rules, valuation bases, and what insurers ask for vary by country and by insurer — treat the specifics here as a starting point and confirm your own policy's terms.

## Why insurers ask for proof — and what happens without it

Two things go wrong at claim time, and both trace back to the same missing homework.

**Under-insurance.** Your "sum insured" is the total value of everything your contents policy covers. Set it too low — which is easy to do from memory — and many insurers apply *average*: if you're insured for half what your contents are actually worth, they can settle a claim at half its value, even a small one. You don't find out until you claim.

**No evidence, no fair payout.** When you claim, the insurer asks what you lost, what it was worth, and how you can show it was yours. Without a record, you're reconstructing a whole household from memory under stress — and every item you can't evidence is one the adjuster can reasonably question or reduce. A documented, valued inventory turns that conversation from "convince me" into "here it is."

An inventory built for insurance solves both: it gives you a defensible total for your sum insured, and item-level evidence for when you actually need to claim.

## What to capture per item so a claim holds up

A photo of a room is a start, but claims are settled item by item. For anything of real value, capture as much of the following as you reasonably can. Each of these maps to a field you can record against the item:

- **Description** — make, model, and enough detail to identify it (a "TV" is weak; a "55-inch [BrandX] OLED, 2023 model" is strong).
- **Purchase date and price** — what you paid and roughly when. This anchors both age and value.
- **Current or replacement value** — what it would cost to replace new today. A single figure *or* a value-range is fine; a range is better than leaving it blank.
- **Serial numbers** — for electronics, appliances, bikes, and tools. These prove the specific item was yours and help police and insurers trace stolen goods.
- **Evidence** — the receipt, a photo of the item, the warranty, a valuation certificate. Attach it to the item so proof and record live together.
- **Insurance status** — whether the item is covered under your standard contents, needs to be *separately specified* on the policy (common for jewellery, watches, bikes, and high-value tech above a single-item limit), or isn't covered at all.

That last point matters more than people expect. Many policies cap what they'll pay on any one item unless you've listed it by name. An engagement ring, a road bike, or a camera kit can sit *technically inside* your contents cover but *practically outside* the single-item limit — so you'd be paid a fraction of its worth. Recording an item's insurance status is how you spot those gaps before a loss, not after.

## Keep it current

An inventory is a living document, not a one-off project. The moment it drifts from what you own, it drifts from what you're covered for. Update it after any significant purchase, a house move, or a renovation, and do a quick full review each year — pinning it to your renewal date is an easy habit. Insure a new item? Add it and set its insurance status the same week. Five minutes of upkeep keeps your sum insured honest and your evidence ready.

## Frequently asked questions

**What counts as proof of ownership for an insurance claim?**
Insurers want evidence that ties an item to you and to a value: receipts or bank/card statements, the original box or manual, photos of the item (ideally showing serial numbers or hallmarks), warranty or registration records, and any professional valuation for jewellery, art, or watches. No single document is mandatory, but the more you can show, the smoother the claim. The practical move is to attach that evidence to each item now — while you still have it — rather than hunting for it after a loss.

**How much contents cover do I need?**
Enough to replace everything you own at today's prices — this is your "sum insured". Most people underestimate it because they value a whole household from memory. The reliable way is to inventory room by room, record a value or value-range per item, and total it up, then check that figure against your policy's contents limit. Remember many policies cap high-value single items (jewellery, bikes, tech) unless you list them separately, so note those too.

**What is the difference between replacement value and actual cash value?**
Replacement (or "new-for-old") value is what it costs to buy the item new today. Actual cash value ("indemnity") is that figure minus wear and depreciation, so it pays less on older belongings. Your payout depends on which basis your policy uses, so it's worth recording roughly what each item would cost to replace new — and checking which basis you're insured on with your broker or insurer.

**How do I prove what I owned after a fire or theft if the evidence burned or was stolen too?**
This is exactly why a contents inventory should live somewhere separate from the items themselves. If your receipts, photos, and the belongings are all in the house, a fire can take the proof along with the property. Keeping a valued inventory with photos and receipts stored off the premises — for example in an app you can open from anywhere — means the evidence survives even when the contents don't.

**Do I need to update my home inventory, and how often?**
Yes. An inventory is only as good as it is current. Update it after any big purchase, a house move, a renovation, or when you insure a new high-value item — and do a quick full review once a year, ideally when your policy renews. That keeps your sum insured matched to what you actually own, so you're neither under-insured nor paying for cover you no longer need.
