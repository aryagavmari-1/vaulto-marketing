If you're reading this because you're worried about a large tax bill landing on your family, start with the reassuring part: **most estates in the UK pay no inheritance tax at all.** The tax only applies to the value of an estate *above* certain tax-free thresholds — so the first question isn't "how much will we owe?" but "are we anywhere near the threshold in the first place?"

This is a plain explainer of how the numbers work: the thresholds, the rate, the spousal transfer, and a simple worked example so you can see the arithmetic. It's written UK-first, because the rules are specific to where you live. Rules vary by country and change over time, so treat everything here as the current UK position, not a permanent one — and if you want the strategies for *reducing* what an estate might owe, our [inheritance tax planning guide](/blog/inheritance-tax-planning/) is the companion to this one.

## The thresholds: what you can pass on tax-free

Two allowances do most of the work. Both are set by the government, and both have been frozen for several years — so the figures below are the current position for the **2026/27 tax year**, and you should confirm the live number before relying on it, because frozen bands can be unfrozen or changed at any budget.

**The nil-rate band.** This is the baseline allowance — commonly **£325,000** per person — and an estate worth less than it owes nothing, whatever it's made up of. Everyone gets it; it's the floor the whole calculation sits on.

**The residence nil-rate band.** On top of the baseline, a home left to your **direct descendants** unlocks a further allowance — commonly **£175,000**. "Direct descendants" means children or grandchildren, and it includes stepchildren, adopted, and foster children. This one comes with strings: it's lost if the home goes to a sibling or a friend rather than your line, and it shrinks for bigger estates — currently starting to reduce once an estate is worth more than £2,000,000. Where it does apply, you stack it on top of the baseline band.

Put together, a single person leaving their home to their children can often pass on up to **£500,000** (£325,000 + £175,000) before any inheritance tax is due. For couples, the number can be far higher — which brings us to the rate.

## The rate, and how the taxable amount is worked out

The headline rate is **40%**, and this is the part people most often misread: it applies **only to the amount above the thresholds**, not to the whole estate. If your allowances come to £500,000 and your estate is worth £600,000, the 40% falls on the £100,000 excess — not the £600,000.

Two things change the sum in most families' favour:

- **The spousal (and civil-partner) transfer.** Anything left to a husband, wife, or civil partner generally passes free of inheritance tax, so the first death typically produces no bill. What most people miss is that the *unused* portion of the first partner's allowances carries over to the survivor — which is how a married couple can end up passing on as much as **£1,000,000** between them before the tax bites.
- **The reduced charity rate.** If at least 10% of the net estate is left to charity, the rate on the rest can drop from 40% to **36%**.

### A simple worked example

*Illustrative only — this is not a calculation of your liability, and it uses synthetic figures.*

Say a married couple's combined estate is worth **£1,300,000**, and on the second death their home (worth £450,000) passes to their children. Roughly, the arithmetic looks like this:

| Step | Amount |
| --- | --- |
| Combined nil-rate band (£325,000 × 2) | £650,000 |
| Combined residence nil-rate band (£175,000 × 2) | £350,000 |
| **Total tax-free allowances** | **£1,000,000** |
| Estate value | £1,300,000 |
| **Taxable amount** (£1,300,000 − £1,000,000) | **£300,000** |
| **Estimated tax at 40%** | **£120,000** |

Change any input and the answer changes: if the home didn't pass to descendants, the residence allowance wouldn't apply; if the estate were over £2,000,000, that allowance would start tapering away. This is exactly why two estates of the same size can owe very different amounts — and why a rough figure needs your real numbers, not a rule of thumb.

## When it's paid, and who pays it

Inheritance tax is paid by **the estate**, not by the individual beneficiaries — the executor or personal representative handles it as part of settling the estate, generally before the remainder is distributed. As a rough guide, it's usually due within six months of the end of the month of death, and probate can't complete until the position is sorted.

The genuinely hard part isn't the rate — it's the *valuation*. Working out what's owed means first working out what the estate is actually worth: every property, account, pension, investment, vehicle, and possession, minus the debts against them. Estates get valued wrongly, or slowly, because the information is scattered across drawers, inboxes, and one person's memory. ## Frequently asked questions

### What is the inheritance tax threshold?

In the UK, each person has a tax-free allowance called the nil-rate band — commonly £325,000 for the 2026/27 tax year — below which no inheritance tax is due. There's an additional residence nil-rate band, commonly £175,000, when your main home passes to your children or grandchildren. Both figures are set by the government and have been frozen for several years, so confirm the current figure before you rely on it.

### How much is inheritance tax in the UK?

The headline rate is 40%, and it applies only to the part of an estate that sits above the tax-free thresholds — not the whole estate. So an estate worth exactly the threshold owes nothing, and an estate above it is taxed at 40% on the excess only. A reduced rate of 36% can apply if you leave at least 10% of the net estate to charity. Rates and thresholds are set by the government and change over time, so treat any figure as the current position rather than a permanent one.

### Do you pay inheritance tax between spouses?

Generally no. Anything left to a husband, wife, or civil partner passes free of inheritance tax, so the first death usually produces no bill. The unused part of the first partner's allowances then carries over to the survivor, which is why a married couple can often pass on up to £1,000,000 between them before inheritance tax applies. The rules are specific and depend on your circumstances, so confirm them for your situation.

### Does the residence nil-rate band apply to me?

The residence nil-rate band is an extra allowance that applies when your main home passes to direct descendants — children, grandchildren, and some others such as stepchildren. It doesn't apply if the home goes to a sibling, a friend, or most other relatives, and it tapers away for larger estates (currently those worth over £2,000,000). Because the rules are conditional, it's worth confirming whether it applies to you rather than assuming it does.
