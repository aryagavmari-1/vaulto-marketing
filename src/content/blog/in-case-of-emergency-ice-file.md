It's a Tuesday. You're in an ambulance, or a hospital bed, or simply unreachable for a few days — nothing you planned, nothing anyone saw coming. Back home, your partner is trying to hold everything together and hitting walls: which account does the mortgage come out of, is there a life insurance policy and with whom, who picks up the kids on Thursday, what's your GP's name, does the hospital need to know about that medication you take?

None of that is about who inherits what. It's about the **first few days** — the acute stretch where you're still here but can't help, and the people around you have to keep the household running without the one person who normally holds it all. An **in-case-of-emergency (ICE) file** is built for exactly that stretch: not a full account of everything you own, but the sharp handful of things someone needs to act *today*.

This is written UK-first, but the idea travels; specific rules on medical consent, access, and what a partner can do on your behalf vary by country, so treat the details as a starting point.

## The ICE file vs. the full binder — two different jobs

It's worth being clear up front, because these get confused. An ICE file and a [family emergency binder](/blog/family-emergency-binder-what-to-include/) are not the same document, and one isn't a shorter draft of the other:

- A **family emergency binder** is the comprehensive record — every account, pension, property, policy, and document your family would need over the weeks and months after something serious. It's a reference. It's meant to be complete.
- An **ICE file** is the emergency-room version. It's deliberately short, because it's used under stress, fast, by someone who isn't you. It answers "what does this person need to act in the first 48 hours," and nothing more.

Build the binder for completeness; build the ICE file for speed. If you only ever do one, do the ICE file — it's the part that fails soonest when it's missing.

## What actually goes in an ICE file

Keep it to what someone genuinely needs in the acute window. Five short sections is plenty — and notice none of these is "a full list of what we own." That's the binder's job; the ICE file points to it, it doesn't reproduce it.

**1. You, medically.** The basics a paramedic, A&E nurse, or worried partner would need: known allergies, ongoing conditions, current medications and doses, your GP surgery, and your NHS number if you have it to hand. This is the section that earns its place in the first hour, before anything financial matters at all.

**2. Who to call, in order.** Not a directory — the two or three people who actually need to know first, ranked. Your partner or next of kin, then whoever backs them up, then the one professional who matters early (often the GP, sometimes an employer). A name and a number against each, and a word on why they're on the list.

**3. Keeping the household running this week.** The bit most emergency advice skips, because it's not about death — it's about continuity. Which account the mortgage, rent, and core direct debits come out of, so nothing bounces while you're out of action. Who collects or cares for the children, and who to tell at their school. Pets, carers, a dependent relative. Your employer, and who should call them. None of this needs balances — it needs "here's how normal life keeps ticking for a fortnight."

**4. Where the real record lives.** This is the one line that connects the ICE file to everything else: where your family finds the full picture — the accounts, the policies, the will, the deeds — when the acute moment turns into the longer job. You don't re-list it all here; you point to it. If you keep a [family emergency binder](/blog/family-emergency-binder-what-to-include/) or a private digital record, this is where you say so and how to reach it.

**5. How someone actually gets in.** An ICE file the right person can't act on is just a nicely organised dead end. Decide now who that person is, and make sure they know how they'd reach what they need — a conversation you've had, an invite you've already sent, an export you've agreed on. More on this below, because it's the part people get wrong most often.

For the wishes, guardianship intentions, and full document list that sit *beyond* the acute window, our guide on [what your family needs to know if something happens to you](/blog/what-your-family-needs-to-know/) and our [checklist of important family documents to keep](/blog/important-family-documents-to-keep/) go deeper than an ICE file should.

## The access problem: who can act while you can't

Here's the trap. You can write a perfect ICE file and still leave your family stuck, because writing down *where* things are doesn't grant anyone the ability to *reach* them. This is sharper for an ICE file than a binder, because the acute emergency is often the living kind — you're incapacitated, not gone — and there's no probate process to eventually sort it out. Someone needs to act this week.

Two shortcuts people reach for both backfire: writing a master password on paper defeats the point of having one, and a "just in case" shared login means several people are on one account with no way to tell who did what. The honest version is deliberate, and set up in advance:

- **Bring someone in while you're both here.** In Vaulto you can invite a specific person into a shared capture session — as capturer, reviewer, or viewer — so they can see and help build the record alongside you. It's scoped to that session, not a key to your whole account, and you set it up on purpose.
- **Agree a handover copy.** You can export everything you've recorded, including your files, as a set your family or solicitor can use directly — a copy handed over on your timing, not a live login into your account.

And be precise about what Vaulto deliberately does **not** do: there's no switch that unlocks your account automatically if you're incapacitated, and none that does it on death. Access is something you arrange ahead of time and on purpose. If your plan quietly assumes someone can "just get in" the day they need to, that assumption is the gap — close it now by deciding who that person is and how they'd actually reach things.

## Where to keep it so it's found in the moment

An ICE file only works if the person looking can find it fast, under stress, without you. A note buried in your phone behind a passcode nobody else knows fails at the first hurdle. A page in a drawer works only if the right person knows the drawer.

The practical answer is a mix: something physical and obvious for the truly immediate medical basics (a card in a wallet, a note the ambulance crew can find), backed by a private, organised record for everything else. That digital record should be genuinely private — a spreadsheet on a shared drive or a note synced to every phone in the house isn't safer than paper, it's just exposed in a different way.

## Keep it current — the ICE file goes stale fastest

Of everything you'll write down, the ICE file is the one that has to be right, because it's used with no time to double-check. A medication you stopped taking, a school you've changed, an emergency contact who's moved away — these are exactly the details that shift, and a wrong one sends help in the wrong direction at the worst moment.

Treat it as two small habits: a quick skim once a year on a date you'll remember, and a one-line update the moment something in it changes. It's a five-minute file by design — keeping it current should be five minutes too.
