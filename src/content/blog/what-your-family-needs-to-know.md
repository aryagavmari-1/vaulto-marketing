Most families don't get caught out because there was no will. They get caught out because, will or not, nobody could find the pieces. The pension from a job three roles ago. The life insurance policy bought when the first child arrived. The name of the accountant. The password to the email address every other account resets through. Even in an organised household, the person who *knows where everything is* is usually one person — and this checklist is about what happens when that person is suddenly unavailable.

This isn't a legal to-do list, and it isn't about death admin for someone who's already gone. It's the proactive version: the information, accounts, and wishes to gather and leave organised **now**, while you're the one who knows it all, so your family isn't left guessing later. Think of it as the "in case something happens" file you hope nobody ever has to open.

One honest note up front, because this topic invites a myth: no app, service, or tool can go out and *find* your estate for you. There is nothing that scans the world's institutions and assembles your accounts into a neat list. Someone has to write it down — and that someone is you, now, in a quiet hour, rather than your family later, under stress. This guide is that writing-down, made as painless as we can make it.

It's written UK-first, but the principles travel; specific rules on wills, probate, and access after death vary by country, so treat the specifics as a starting point and confirm your own.

## Why a will on its own still leaves your family guessing

A will is essential, and if you don't have one, that's the first job — our [will planning checklist](/blog/will-planning-checklist/) walks through it. But a will answers *who gets what*. It rarely answers *where is it, how do I reach it, and who do I call*. Probate can't distribute a pension nobody knew existed, and an executor can't close accounts they can't locate.

So the months of hunting happen in the gap between the two documents: the will that decides, and the missing map that would have made it findable. Families end up writing to every bank on the off-chance, digging through drawers for policy numbers, and locked out of the one email account that could have reset the others. (If you're on the receiving end of that search right now, our guide on [how to find a deceased person's assets](/blog/how-to-find-a-deceased-persons-assets/) is written for you — this post is about making sure your family never needs it.)

The fix is to build the map yourself, while you can, and keep it somewhere the right person can reach. Here's what goes in it.

## The checklist: what to gather now

Work through these six categories. You don't have to finish in one sitting — the point is to get it out of your head and into one organised place, then keep it current.

### 1. The money map

A plain list of what you own and owe, and — crucially — *where each thing lives*, not the logins in plaintext. For each entry, note the institution, the type of account or asset, and roughly where the paperwork sits:

- **Accounts** — current, savings, pensions (including old workplace ones), ISAs, and investments. Name the provider; you don't need balances to the penny.
- **Property** — homes, land, anything with a deed or a mortgage.
- **Debts** — mortgage, loans, credit cards, anything owed. Debts don't disappear; your family needs to know what's outstanding and to whom.
- **Insurance** — life, critical illness, and any policies that pay out. These are among the most commonly missed, because nobody's looking at them month to month.

To be clear about how this list gets built: you assemble it by hand. Vaulto and tools like it do **not** connect to your bank, sync balances, or discover accounts you've forgotten — you add each one yourself. That's slower than a magic scan, but it's the only version that's actually true, and it means your banking credentials never leave your control. For the wider picture of everything a household owns and owes, our pillar guide on [what a family asset inventory is](/blog/what-is-a-family-asset-inventory/) goes deeper.

### 2. Important documents and where they live

The document itself matters less than *knowing it exists and where to find the original*. Note the location of:

- The **will** (and the name of the solicitor or where the signed original is stored).
- **Property deeds** and mortgage paperwork.
- **Insurance policies** and pension statements.
- **Tax records**, National Insurance number, and any recent returns.
- Birth, marriage, and passport documents.

A short "where it lives" line against each one saves days. Our checklist of the [important family documents to keep](/blog/important-family-documents-to-keep/) covers the full set and how long to hold each.

### 3. Your digital life

More of an estate now lives online than most people realise — and it's the part a will almost never covers. Capture:

- **The password manager** you use (and how a trusted person can reach it through *its* recovery or legacy feature — see the FAQ; never write master passwords down in plaintext).
- **Key accounts** — primary email above all, because most other accounts reset through it.
- **Subscriptions and recurring payments** that will keep charging until someone stops them.
- Any **photos, files, or accounts** with sentimental or practical value.

This is a checklist in its own right; our [digital estate planning checklist](/blog/digital-estate-planning-checklist/) handles the detail, including legacy-contact settings on the big platforms.

### 4. Key contacts

A short list of the professionals and people your family would otherwise have to identify from scratch: your **solicitor**, **accountant**, **financial adviser**, **executor**, and anyone holding **power of attorney**. A name and a phone number against each turns a week of detective work into a single afternoon of calls.

### 5. Wishes that aren't in the will

The things people most want to say, and most often leave unsaid: **funeral preferences**, any **messages** for specific people, and your **intentions for guardianship** of children or dependants. Write them down so your family isn't guessing at what you'd have wanted.

One important caveat: notes like these are not always legally binding — guardianship and funeral wishes in particular may need to be handled formally to carry legal weight. Record them here so your intentions are *known*, and speak to a qualified professional about making the ones that matter legally effective. This checklist captures wishes; it doesn't make them law.

### 6. Access: who gets it, and how it stays current

A perfect file nobody can open is no file at all. Decide **who** should be able to reach this — usually a partner and your executor — and make sure they know it exists and roughly where. Then set a rhythm to **keep it current**: a quick review once a year, plus an update whenever something big changes (a new policy, a house move, a closed account). An out-of-date map sends people down the wrong roads.

## How to actually store and share this

So where does all of this live? The two default answers both fail at the worst moment. A **paper binder** in a drawer can burn, flood, or simply not be found — and it's out of date the day after you write it. A **file on your laptop or a note on your phone** is worse if it holds passwords in plaintext, because a lost or compromised device hands everything over at once.

What you actually want is one **organised, private place** where each record sits alongside the documents and details that belong to it, that a trusted person can be brought into deliberately when the time comes, and that's easy enough to update that it doesn't drift out of date. Living, not dusty. Private, not exposed. Reachable, not locked inside one person's head.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is this a substitute for a will?**
No. A will is a legal document that says who inherits what and who carries out your wishes; this checklist is the practical map that helps them actually do it. The two work together — a will decides, an organised information file makes it findable. Nothing here is legal advice, and none of it replaces a properly drafted, witnessed will. If you don't have one, or your circumstances have changed, speak to a qualified solicitor or estate professional. Keep the will itself in the "important documents" section of your file and note where the signed original lives.

**Where should I keep this information?**
Somewhere one organised, private, and accessible to the people who'll need it — which usually rules out a single paper binder that can burn, flood, or go missing, and also rules out a plaintext note on your laptop. The practical answer is one private place you control, where each record sits with the documents and details that belong to it, and where a trusted person can be brought in when the time comes. The test is simple: if you couldn't explain it today, could your partner or executor find it without you?

**Should I write down my passwords?**
Not in plaintext — not in a notebook, a spreadsheet, or a "passwords" note on your phone. Use a reputable password manager, which stores all your logins behind one strong master password and can be set up to pass access to a trusted person if something happens to you. In your information file, record *that you use a password manager and which one*, and make sure your emergency contact can reach the master password through the manager's own recovery or legacy process — never by reading it off a page.

**Who should have access to it?**
The people who would actually need it: usually your partner or spouse, and whoever you've named as executor or power of attorney. Some people also loop in an adult child or a sibling as a backup. The point isn't to broadcast your finances — it's to make sure at least one trusted person can find the map if you can't hand it to them. Decide who, tell them it exists and roughly where, and choose a tool that lets you bring someone in deliberately rather than leaving your login lying around.
