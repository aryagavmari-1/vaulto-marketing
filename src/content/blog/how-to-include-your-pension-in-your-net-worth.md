If you've ever calculated your net worth by adding up your savings, your home equity, and your investments, there's a good chance you left out the single largest thing you own: your pension.

It's an easy thing to skip. A pension doesn't arrive as a number in a bank app — it sits in a portal you rarely log into, or it's a promise from a scheme you haven't thought about since you left that job. But it's still an asset, often the biggest one after property, and leaving it out doesn't make your finances simpler — it makes your net-worth figure wrong.

Here's how to find a real number for the two main types of UK pension, and how to record it so it doesn't quietly go stale. If you haven't calculated the rest of your household's net worth yet, start with [how to calculate your family's net worth](/blog/how-to-calculate-your-familys-net-worth/); this post picks up the one part that guide leaves out.

## Should a pension count in your net worth?

Yes — a pension is an asset, the same as a savings account or a share portfolio, and your household's [net worth formula](/blog/household-net-worth-formula/) is simply assets minus liabilities. Leaving a pension out doesn't make the calculation more conservative; it makes it inaccurate, sometimes by a large margin, since for many people in their 40s and 50s a pension pot is worth more than the equity in their home.

The reason people leave it out usually isn't a considered decision — it's that a pension is harder to see. It's illiquid (you generally can't access it before 55, rising to 57 from 2028), it doesn't sit in an account you check, and for one common type there's no obvious "balance" at all. That's a genuine complication for *how* you value it — covered below — but it's not a reason to exclude it from the total.

## Defined contribution (DC) pensions: the easy case

If you have a workplace or personal pension where contributions build up a pot — most private-sector pensions since auto-enrolment — this is the straightforward one. The value is simply the current pot value shown on your latest statement or provider portal.

To find it:

1. Log into your pension provider's portal, or find your most recent annual statement.
2. Note the current pot value and the date it's valid as of — pension statements often lag by a few weeks or months, so the figure is a recent snapshot, not a live number.
3. If you've moved jobs a few times, check for old workplace pensions you may have lost track of — the government's [free pension tracing service](https://www.gov.uk/find-pension-contact-details) can help locate them using an old employer's name.
4. Add up every DC pot separately if you hold more than one; don't estimate a combined figure.

That total goes straight into your net worth as an asset, alongside your other savings and investments.

## Defined benefit (DB) / final salary pensions: the harder case

DB pensions — increasingly rare outside the public sector, but still common for anyone with older service — don't have a pot. Instead, they promise you an income for life, calculated from your salary and years of service. That makes "what is it worth today" a genuinely different question, and there's no single right answer.

Two common approximations:

- **Ask your scheme for a cash equivalent transfer value (CETV).** This is the closest thing to an official lump-sum figure — what the scheme calculates it would cost to buy out your pension entitlement. Most schemes will provide one on request, sometimes once a year for free.
- **Use a rough multiple.** A commonly used rule of thumb multiplies your expected annual pension by somewhere around 20–25, though the right multiple varies by scheme, your age, and current interest rates.

Both are estimates, not facts — a CETV in particular moves with interest rates and can shift meaningfully within a year, so treat it as a snapshot rather than a fixed number. **This is general information, not financial advice.** For a figure you actually want to rely on for a decision, ask your scheme administrator or a regulated financial adviser rather than relying on a rule of thumb.

## Does the State Pension count?

Generally, no. Net worth is a snapshot of what you hold and what you owe — assets with a transferable or realisable value today. The State Pension is neither: it's a future income entitlement based on your National Insurance record, with no transferable balance and nothing to cash in. Most net-worth calculations leave it out for exactly that reason.

It's still worth checking your [State Pension forecast](https://www.gov.uk/check-state-pension) for planning purposes — just keep it separate from your net-worth total so you don't end up double-counting a promise of future income as if it were a current asset.

## Recording it so the number stays current

A pension value is a snapshot, not a live figure — so what you're really recording is *the value, and when it was true*:

- Note the statement date alongside the value, not just the number on its own.
- Revisit at least once a year — pension statements update annually in most cases, and CETVs go stale faster than DC pot values.
- If you hold pensions from multiple employers, record each one individually rather than as a single combined guess.

Treated this way, your net worth stops being a one-off calculation and becomes a figure you can trust because you know exactly how current it is.

## Frequently asked questions

**Do I include my partner's pension in our net worth?**
That depends on whether you're calculating a household figure or an individual one. If you're building a joint picture of what you and your partner own together, include both pensions. If you're working out your own individual net worth — for a mortgage application, a divorce, or your own planning — count only your own. Keep the two separate rather than mixing them, since it's easy to lose track of which figure you're actually looking at.
