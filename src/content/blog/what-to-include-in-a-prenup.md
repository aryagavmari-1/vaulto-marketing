Prenup conversations tend to stall in the same place. Not on the difficult questions — on the basic one. Neither person has a clear picture of what they actually own, so the discussion stays vague, the solicitor's first meeting turns into an admin session, and everything takes longer than it needed to.

The list is the part you can prepare in advance. This guide covers what goes on it, category by category, what people routinely forget, and how to put yours together without it swallowing a month.

It's written UK-first — rules vary by country, and in the US by state — but the categories and the method travel.

## What a prenup actually asks of you

A prenuptial agreement has two halves, and only one of them is your job.

**The terms** — what the agreement says about property brought in, the family home, a business, an inheritance, children from an earlier relationship — are drafting work. Each of you needs your own solicitor for that; one solicitor can't act for both.

**The disclosure** comes first: the financial picture each of you gives the other before any of those terms are agreed. The phrase that matters is **full and frank**. Neither of you can properly weigh an agreement without knowing what the other one has, which is why disclosure isn't paperwork attached to the process — it's the foundation the agreement stands on.

As general information: in England and Wales a prenup isn't automatically binding on a court. Since the Supreme Court's 2010 decision in *Radmacher v Granatino*, courts will generally give effect to an agreement entered into freely and with a full appreciation of its implications, unless it would be unfair to hold the couple to it. In practice that points at independent legal advice on each side, complete disclosure, no pressure, and signing well ahead of the wedding — the Law Commission's 2014 review recommended at least 28 days, and that's the figure most solicitors work to; several months is more comfortable. Scotland differs, and US states have their own rules. Your solicitor confirms what applies to you.

One thing worth saying early, because it's what a lot of people are quietly wondering: this is about documenting **your own** finances. No app can find, aggregate or verify what the other person has. Disclosure is something two people give each other, and if you have real concerns about what you're being given, that's a conversation for your solicitor rather than a job for software.

## What to include: the asset list, category by category

Work through categories rather than memory. Memory is what leaves the dormant savings account off the list.

- **Property and land** — the home you live in or plan to buy, plus anything else: a flat you kept from before, a share in a family property, land, a place overseas. Note who's on the title and who funded the deposit.
- **Current and savings accounts** — every account, ISA and cash holding, including sole accounts and the ones that predate the relationship.
- **Pensions** — often the largest asset after a home and the most commonly missed, because it doesn't feel like money yet. Current and old workplace schemes, plus any personal pension, at the latest statement value.
- **Investments and shares** — funds, bonds, shares in a private company, employee share and option schemes, crypto. Where a scheme vests over time, note the dates.
- **Business interests** — a company you own or co-own, a partnership, self-employment. Say who else holds a stake and whether a shareholders' agreement exists; an agreement that touches a business has to work alongside those documents. Valuation is a professional job — listing it accurately is yours.
- **Vehicles** — cars, a motorbike, a boat, a caravan, anything with a logbook or title.
- **Valuables and contents** — jewellery, watches, art, instruments, collections, high-end tech.
- **Digital and intellectual assets** — domains, a monetised account or channel, royalties, intellectual property.
- **Inheritances and family gifts, received or expected** — often the whole reason a couple wants an agreement: money or property a family intends to keep in the family. Include what's already come to you and, in general terms, what you have reason to expect.
- **Assets held abroad** — a foreign account, a property overseas, a pension from a job in another country. Cross-border assets complicate an agreement, so flag them early.

If a category is empty for you, tick it off and move on. The point of a checklist is being able to say you considered it.

## What you owe counts too

An asset list without liabilities isn't disclosure — it's half a picture, and usually the flattering half. Include the mortgage, personal and student loans, credit cards, car finance, family or help-to-buy loans, business borrowing you've personally guaranteed, and any tax owed. Record the current balance and roughly when the debt was taken on.

Debts change the real shape of what each of you brings in, sometimes more than the assets do. How the agreement treats them is a question for your solicitors — but they can only work with what's on the list.

## The bits people forget

Almost every incomplete list is missing something from this handful:

- A workplace pension from a job you left years ago.
- An ISA or savings account you opened and stopped thinking about.
- A share or save-as-you-earn scheme from a previous employer.
- A car on finance, which is an asset and a debt at once.
- A foreign account left open after living or working abroad.
- A family loan with no paperwork — money lent to you, or by you.
- An expected inheritance that everyone in the family knows about and nobody has written down.

## How to build the list without it taking a month

Two passes, ideally in one sitting, because each finds what the other misses.

**Pass one, account by account.** Open your banking app, your email and your document drawer, and log every provider you see — accounts, pensions, investments, policies. This is the sweep that catches invisible assets, the ones with no physical form.

**Pass two, room by room.** Walk the house and log the physical and proof-bearing things: valuables, electronics, tools, the art on the wall, the documents in the filing cabinet. Photograph as you go — it's faster than typing and doubles as evidence.

Then record six things per entry. Points four and five are the ones specific to a prenup:

1. What it is, and its category
2. Roughly what it's worth — with the date and basis of that figure
3. Whose name it's in
4. **When and how you came by it** — before the relationship or during it; earned, inherited or gifted
5. Whether it's been mixed with shared money — a pre-relationship savings pot used for a joint deposit, an inherited sum paid into a shared account
6. Where the proof lives

Point four does a lot of work. Agreements usually turn on the difference between what each of you brought in and what you build together, so *when* and *how* is often the most important fact about an asset. Mixing doesn't disqualify anything — it's simply something the solicitors need to know rather than discover later.

On values: a rough figure with a date beats a precise figure with no date, and both beat a blank. If you're unsure, record a range and move on.

## You each build your own list

Disclosure runs both ways, so make it symmetrical: same categories, same columns, same deadline, two separate lists. Two complete lists you then compare openly beat one shared spreadsheet that neither of you owns and neither quite trusts.

That's how Vaulto works too — a vault has a **single owner**, so you'd each keep your own and exchange exports rather than pooling everything into one account. You can invite someone into a **capture session** with a defined role — capturer, reviewer or viewer — if you want help adding or checking entries, and remove them afterwards. That's help with your list, not joint ownership of it, and it's separate from the formal disclosure your solicitors exchange.

It also helps to separate *listing* from *deciding*. The list describes what exists. What happens to any of it is a later conversation, with advice in the room. Trying to do both at once is where these evenings go wrong.
