## Why many people won't link their bank just to track net worth

Most popular net-worth and budgeting trackers work the same way: you connect your bank accounts, and an aggregator logs in on your behalf to pull your balances and transactions automatically. It's genuinely convenient, and for plenty of people it's worth it.

But a lot of people simply prefer not to. The reasons are practical, not paranoid:

- **You're sharing access you can't easily see.** To auto-sync, you grant a third party read access to your live accounts. Even when that's done carefully, it's more exposure than some people want just to answer "what are we worth?"
- **Convenience you may not need.** Auto-syncing shines for categorizing daily spending. But net worth isn't a live feed — it's a snapshot you check now and then. You don't need continuous access to your accounts to know roughly what you own and owe.
- **It's a preference, and that's allowed.** Some people are fine linking accounts; others aren't. Neither is wrong.

To be clear: this isn't a claim that bank-linking apps are unsafe or that aggregators leak data — reputable ones invest heavily in security. It's simply that linking your bank is a *choice*, and if you'd rather not make it, you can still track your net worth completely.

## The manual method: what you own minus what you owe

The whole formula is one line:

> **Net worth = everything you own (assets) − everything you owe (liabilities).**

That's it. The math is trivial; the work is gathering an honest list of both sides. If you want the full walkthrough — how to value a home you're not selling, what counts as an asset, how to handle unknowns — read [how to calculate your family's net worth](/blog/how-to-calculate-your-familys-net-worth/) and the plain-English [household net-worth formula](/blog/household-net-worth-formula/). No bank connection appears anywhere in either method.

Doing it by hand has a quiet upside beyond privacy: because you enter each item yourself, you actually *look* at what you own. People routinely surface a forgotten pension, an old policy, or a paid-off asset the moment they sit down and list things — something an auto-sync feed silently skips because it only sees linked accounts.

## What to include (without reinventing the list)

You don't need every receipt — you need the big pieces on each side of the ledger. At a glance:

- **What you own:** your home and any property, vehicles, cash and savings, investments and pensions, and high-value valuables.
- **What you owe:** your mortgage balance, loans, credit-card balances, and any other outstanding debts.

The [net-worth guide](/blog/how-to-calculate-your-familys-net-worth/) breaks each of these down with how to value them, so we won't repeat the full category list here. ## Keeping a manual tracker current without it becoming a chore

The usual knock on manual tracking is that it goes stale. That's a real risk — but it's a workflow problem, not a reason to link your bank. Three habits keep a by-hand tracker honest:

1. **Don't retype what you can capture.** The slow part of manual entry is typing numbers off a statement. If your tool lets you photograph a statement or document and pull the details from the image, that removes most of the friction — you snap, check, and save. (In Vaulto this photo-read step is optional and per file; more on that below.)
2. **Set a quarterly rhythm.** You don't need daily updates. A quick pass every three months — plus a check after any big change like a house purchase, a new loan, or an inheritance — keeps the number trustworthy.
3. **Keep it in one place, not five.** A figure scattered across a spreadsheet, a notes app, and a drawer of statements is the figure that goes stale. One home for the whole picture is what makes the quarterly pass a five-minute job instead of a scavenger hunt.

## Manual vs. bank-linking trackers — the honest trade-offs

Neither approach is universally "better." They optimise for different things:

| | Manual tracking (no linking) | Bank-linking tracker |
|---|---|---|
| **Setup** | Enter or photograph items yourself | Connect accounts once |
| **Staying current** | You update on a rhythm (e.g. quarterly) | Balances auto-refresh |
| **Access shared** | No banking credentials shared | Third-party read access to live accounts |
| **Best for** | A private snapshot of what you own and owe | Live spend tracking and auto-categorising |
| **Effort over time** | A little, on your schedule | Low, once connected |

If your goal is day-to-day spend categorisation, auto-syncing earns its keep. If your goal is a clear, private picture of what your household is worth — and where the records live — manual tracking gets you there without linking a thing. Many people want the second and reach for a tool built for the first, then feel uneasy about the access they granted.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is manual net-worth tracking accurate?

Yes, as accurate as you make it. Bank-linking pulls live balances automatically, but net worth doesn't need live precision — it's a snapshot you revisit on a rhythm, not a live feed. For everyday assets like cash and investments, use your latest statement; for a home or a car, a realistic current estimate is enough. Round where you have to. A hand-kept figure you update each quarter is far more accurate than a linked tracker you stopped trusting, or a spreadsheet you abandoned.

### Do I have to connect my bank to track net worth?

No. Connecting your bank is a convenience feature some trackers offer so balances update automatically — it is never a requirement for knowing what you own and owe. You can list your accounts, property, and debts by entering or photographing them yourself, without handing any app your banking credentials. If linking accounts makes you uneasy, choose a tool that doesn't require it. Vaulto doesn't: there's no bank login, and no bank-account linking.

### Is a spreadsheet safer than an app for tracking net worth?

A spreadsheet keeps you off bank-linking, which some people prefer — but "offline file" isn't the same as "safe." A spreadsheet on one laptop can be lost with that laptop, has no real access control if you share the link, and tends to go stale because updating it is a chore. The safer question for any option — spreadsheet or app — is: who else can see it, is it encrypted, and can you get your data out? Judge each tool on those points rather than assuming a file is automatically safer.

### How often should I update a manual net-worth tracker?

A quarterly pass is plenty for most families — a quick update of balances and values four times a year keeps the number honest without it becoming a chore. Also update after big changes: buying or selling a home, taking on or clearing a loan, or a large gift or inheritance. The goal is a number you trust when you look at it, not a live ticker.
