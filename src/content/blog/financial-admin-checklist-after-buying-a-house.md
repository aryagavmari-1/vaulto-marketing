Completion day gets all the attention — the call from your conveyancer, the keys, the first night in the house. What nobody hands you at completion is the list of admin that opens up behind it: a new asset to record, a new liability to track, paperwork that needs a home, and a handful of things that quietly need updating now that your address and your balance sheet have both changed.

None of it is urgent the way exchange and completion were. That's exactly why it's easy to let it slide — and why, a year later, people find themselves digging through email for a mortgage offer or guessing at what their title deeds even look like. Some of the items below genuinely need doing in the first few weeks; others just need a home so they're not lost by the time you need them. Here's the difference, and where to put everything so it doesn't have to be found twice.

## The checklist: what to sort in the first weeks

A few of these have real deadlines. Buildings insurance needs to start from completion, not moving-in day — most conveyancers will already have flagged this, but it's worth confirming the policy is actually live. The rest are less urgent but still time-limited in practice: the longer paperwork sits unfiled and a net-worth figure sits out of date, the more likely it is to get lost or forgotten entirely.

- **Buildings insurance** — this needs to be in place from the moment you legally own the property, not from moving-in day. Most conveyancers will flag this before completion, but confirm the policy is active and the certificate is saved somewhere you can find it.
- **Title deeds / title register and TR1** — most UK property has been registered electronically with HM Land Registry since 1990, so you may not get a physical deed. What you will get from your conveyancer is a copy of the official [title register](https://www.gov.uk/search-property-information-land-registry) and the TR1 transfer form. File both.
- **Mortgage offer and account details** — the formal offer document, your account number, and your first statement once payments start.
- **Update your net worth** — the property is a new asset, the mortgage is a new liability. Both sides need updating, not just one.
- **Will and life cover** — a mortgage is usually the largest debt most people ever take on. If you have a will, check it still reflects reality; if you have life insurance, check the level of cover still makes sense against the new balance.
- **Standing orders and council tax** — redirect anything tied to your old address, and set up council tax at the new one.

## Update your net-worth picture

A house purchase moves both sides of your household balance sheet at once, which is what makes it easy to record wrong. The property itself is an asset — worth roughly what you paid until you have reason to think otherwise. The mortgage is a liability at the amount you borrowed, and it changes every month as you pay it down, even in years when it doesn't feel like it's moving.

The mistake worth avoiding is recording only the asset. A house without its mortgage attached isn't your net worth — it's a bigger number than the one you actually have. Say you bought for £320,000 with a £250,000 mortgage: the honest addition to your household net worth is £70,000 (the equity), not £320,000. Add the property alone and every future net-worth check is overstated until someone notices the gap.

If you've already worked out [how to calculate your family's net worth](/blog/how-to-calculate-your-familys-net-worth/) — assets minus liabilities — this is the moment to feed both new figures in and let the total recalculate.

Worth saying plainly: these are figures you set and keep current yourself. You record what you paid for the property and the balance you borrowed, and you update them as you get a formal valuation or pay the mortgage down. Treat the property value as your own working estimate that you keep up to date — not a precise or independently verified figure.

## Where to keep the paperwork

By the time you've completed, you're usually holding (or waiting on) a stack of documents that don't have an obvious home: the title register and TR1, the mortgage offer, your mortgage statements as they arrive, the buildings insurance policy, and any warranties or guarantees that came with the property — a new-build warranty, a boiler service record, a damp-proofing guarantee.

Individually, none of these feel urgent to file properly. Together, they're the paperwork you'll actually need if you remortgage, claim on the insurance, sell, or need to prove something about the property years from now — and the point at which you need one is rarely the point at which you remember where you put it. A boiler warranty matters when the boiler breaks, not when it arrives in an email; a title register matters when a buyer's solicitor asks for it, not when you first read it and set it aside.

[Keeping important documents somewhere findable](/blog/where-to-keep-important-financial-documents/) — rather than split across a solicitor's email, a filing cabinet, and a phone photo — is the difference between a five-minute search and a genuine scramble. That's true of any [important family document](/blog/important-family-documents-to-keep/), and a house purchase is usually the moment the pile gets big enough to notice.

## Frequently asked questions

**What financial admin do I need to do after buying a house?**
In the first few weeks after completion, the main things to sort are: buildings insurance in place from the day you legally own the property, your title deeds (or the digital title register) and TR1 transfer form filed somewhere safe, your mortgage offer and account details recorded, your household net worth updated to include the new asset and the new liability, your will and life cover reviewed against the mortgage, and your standing orders and council tax redirected to the new address. None of it is urgent in the way exchange and completion were — but each item left undone is one more thing to reconstruct later if you ever need it in a hurry.

**Do I need to update my net worth after buying a house?**
Yes, and it changes both sides of the calculation, not just one. The property becomes an asset at roughly what you paid (or a later valuation, if you get one) and the mortgage becomes a liability at the amount you borrowed. Missing either side gives you a wrong number — recording the property without the mortgage overstates what you're worth, and most people update the asset side and forget the liability side entirely. Update both when your net worth is recalculated, and keep the mortgage balance current as you pay it down, since it changes every month even if the headline figure doesn't feel like it.

**Where should I store my house deeds and mortgage documents?**
Since 1990, HM Land Registry has held the legal title record for most UK property electronically, so you may not receive a paper deed at all — what you'll typically get from your conveyancer is a copy of the title register and the TR1 transfer form. Keep those alongside your mortgage offer, your mortgage account statements, and your buildings insurance policy in one place, rather than split across email attachments and a solicitor's letter you filed and forgot. If you ever remortgage, sell, or need to prove ownership, having all of it together — and knowing where "together" is — saves a scramble.
