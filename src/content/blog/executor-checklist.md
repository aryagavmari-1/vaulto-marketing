Being named executor is one of those jobs that arrives without a description. Somebody has died, you are the person the will names, and the questions start immediately: what am I actually supposed to do, in what order, and how do I avoid getting something badly wrong?

Here is the reassuring part. The role is a sequence, not a cliff — roughly ten things, mostly in order, each a small finishable task with a clear "what you'll need" list. You don't have to hold the whole estate in your head on day one.

> *This is general information to help you get organized, not legal, tax, or financial advice. Probate and executor rules vary by country (and between England & Wales, Scotland, and Northern Ireland) and change over time — confirm specifics with a qualified solicitor or probate practitioner.*

This checklist is written UK-first, using the terms you'll meet on GOV.UK and in letters from banks: *probate*, *grant of probate*, *HMRC*, *IHT400*. In Scotland the vocabulary differs — you are an *executor-nominate* if the will names you, an *executor-dative* if the court appoints you, and the equivalent of the grant is called *confirmation*. Northern Ireland has its own process again, as does every country outside the UK.

## The duties of an executor, in order

### 1. Register the death and get certified copies

Registering the death is the first formal step, and in England and Wales it normally happens within five days. The registrar gives you the death certificate — order **several certified copies** while you're there, because most institutions will not accept a photocopy and will want to see an original before they'll tell you anything. Ten is not excessive for an estate with a few banks, a pension and a property.

**What you'll need:** the medical certificate of cause of death, and the person's date and place of birth, address, occupation, and NHS number if you have it.

### 2. Find the will and confirm you're the executor

Locate the signed original, not a copy — check the home paperwork, the solicitor who drafted it, the bank, and the National Will Register. Read it properly and confirm two things: that you are named as executor, and whether anyone is named alongside you.

This is also the moment to decide whether to act at all. You can renounce the role, or reserve power while a co-executor does the work, or stay as executor and instruct a solicitor to handle the administration. All of those are normal. What you should not do is start dealing with the estate and then try to step back.

**What you'll need:** the original will and any codicils, plus the contact details of any co-executors.

### 3. Secure the property and valuables

Before anything else moves, make the estate physically safe. Lock up the property, take meter readings, redirect the post, and remove or secure obviously valuable items. Then **tell the insurer** the property is unoccupied — most home policies restrict or void cover after a period of vacancy, and an unnoticed lapse is one of the more expensive mistakes an executor can make.

**What you'll need:** keys, the home insurance policy, and a note of who now holds what.

### 4. Build the list of assets and liabilities

Now the substantive work starts: a complete list of everything the person owned and owed. Property, bank and savings accounts, pensions, investments, life policies, vehicles, valuables, business interests, and digital assets on one side; mortgage, loans, credit cards, tax owed, and unpaid bills on the other.

Write to every institution asking for the balance **as at the date of death** — that's the figure the estate is valued on, not today's. Keep one running record with a reference for each item and a note of where the paperwork lives.

This step eats the calendar, and it has its own guide: [how to find a deceased person's assets](/blog/how-to-find-a-deceased-persons-assets/) covers where to look and which free tracing services exist.

**What you'll need:** certified death certificates, account and policy numbers, and a folder — physical or digital — that everything lands in.

### 5. Value the estate

A list becomes a valuation when every line has a defensible number against it. Bank and pension figures come from the institutions in writing. Property normally needs an estate agent's or, for anything contested or unusual, a RICS surveyor's valuation. Personal possessions are valued at what they'd fetch on the open market, not what they were insured for or what they cost.

**What you'll need:** date-of-death balances in writing, at least one property valuation, and valuations for any item of real worth.

### 6. Work out whether inheritance tax is due, and whether probate is needed

These are two separate questions and they're easy to conflate. Inheritance tax depends on the estate's value against the available allowances and on who inherits — our [inheritance tax overview](/blog/how-much-is-inheritance-tax/) is a plain-English starting point, and HMRC's reporting requirements sit on their own timetable. Whether you need a grant depends on what the estate holds: institutions set their own thresholds, and jointly held assets often pass outside the estate entirely.

Get this pair right with professional input rather than a rule of thumb. It's the step where a solicitor or probate practitioner most reliably pays for themselves.

**What you'll need:** the completed valuation, details of gifts made in the years before death, and the will's list of beneficiaries.

### 7. Apply for the grant

With the valuation done and any inheritance tax reporting handled, you apply for the grant of probate — the document that proves your authority to deal with the estate. In England and Wales that's an application to HM Courts & Tribunals Service, online or on paper, with a fee. In Scotland you apply for confirmation. You can do it yourself or instruct a practitioner to do it for you.

**What you'll need:** the original will, a death certificate, your inheritance tax reference or confirmation that reporting is complete, and the estate figures.

### 8. Settle the debts and taxes

Once the grant arrives, institutions will release funds to you. Debts and tax are paid **before** anyone inherits, in the order the law sets out. Executors commonly place a statutory notice for creditors — in England and Wales, in The Gazette and a local paper — which protects them against unknown debts surfacing after distribution. The deceased's own income tax position also needs closing off with HMRC.

An executor can be held personally responsible for distributing an estate before its debts are settled. That risk is precisely what the statutory notice and professional advice exist for.

**What you'll need:** the grant, a full list of creditors, and an estate bank account to run payments through.

### 9. Distribute the estate

With debts and tax cleared, you pay the legacies and divide the residue as the will directs. Get a signed receipt from every beneficiary. If anyone is a minor, untraceable, or the will's wording is ambiguous, stop and take advice before paying anything out — this is not a step to improvise.

**What you'll need:** the will's distribution clauses, beneficiary identity and bank details, and a receipt for each payment.

### 10. Keep and share the estate accounts

Finally, produce estate accounts: what came in, what went out, what each beneficiary received. Residuary beneficiaries are entitled to see them, and a clear set of accounts is the best protection against a dispute two years later. Keep the whole file — valuations, correspondence, receipts — for several years after the estate closes.

**What you'll need:** every statement, invoice, and receipt from the administration, and the running record you started at step 4.

## The part that actually takes the time

Nine of those ten steps are procedural. One is a search.

For almost every executor, the hard part isn't the forms — it's finding out what the person owned in the first place. There is no master list. You are working through unopened post, old statements, tax letters, a dormant building-society passbook, an inbox full of paperless statements, and a pension from a job in the 1980s that nobody mentioned. Things surface weeks apart, and each one sends you back to the valuation.

That search is a manual hunt, and no service can shortcut it for you. What helps is a method: work the paper trail in passes, use the free tracing routes for anything that's gone quiet, and keep every confirmed item in one running inventory. ## Frequently asked questions

**Do I always need probate?**
Not always. Whether you need a grant of probate depends on what the estate holds and how it was owned. Small bank balances can often be released without one — each bank sets its own threshold — and assets held as joint tenants usually pass to the surviving owner outside the estate. Property held in the deceased's sole name, or a share held as tenants in common, almost always needs a grant before it can be sold or transferred. The practical route is to write to each institution, tell them the value they hold and ask whether they will release it without a grant, and apply for the grant if any of them requires it. GOV.UK sets out the official test for England and Wales; Scotland and Northern Ireland differ, and a probate practitioner can confirm your case.

**Can an executor also be a beneficiary of the will?**
Yes. It's very common for someone to be both — a surviving spouse or an adult child is often named executor and left a share of the estate. Being a beneficiary doesn't stop you acting. What it does mean is that your two roles have to stay visibly separate: as executor you owe a duty to the estate and to every beneficiary, including yourself, so keep clean estate accounts and take advice before any decision where your personal interest and the estate's interest could pull apart. A separate rule applies to witnesses, not executors: in England and Wales, someone who witnessed the will generally cannot inherit under it. If your situation is anywhere near that line, ask a solicitor.

**How long does an executor have to settle an estate?**
There's no single deadline, and honest answers here are ranges rather than promises. A straightforward estate is often talked about in months rather than weeks; anything with property to sell, a business, overseas assets, or a dispute can run considerably longer. In England and Wales, convention refers to the "executor's year" — beneficiaries are generally not entitled to press for their share within the first twelve months, which reflects how long the work realistically takes rather than a target. Some deadlines are real, though: inheritance tax has its own payment and reporting timetable, and missing it has consequences. Ask a probate practitioner what applies to your estate rather than working to a rule of thumb.

**Can I refuse to act as executor?**
Yes, provided you act before you start. If you haven't yet dealt with the estate's affairs, you can formally renounce the role, which passes it to any substitute executor named in the will or to the next entitled person. You can also reserve power — stepping back while another executor acts, with the option to step in later — or appoint a solicitor to do the work on the estate's behalf while you remain the named executor. What you should not do is start administering the estate and then walk away, because once you've "intermeddled" renouncing gets much harder. If you're unsure, speak to a probate practitioner before you touch anything.

**What if I can't find all the accounts?**
That's the normal experience, not a failure. There is no central database of everything a person owned, so it's a search through post, statements and tax letters, plus free tracing services such as My Lost Account for dormant bank and building-society accounts and the Pension Tracing Service for lost pensions. Keep one running inventory and add to it as things surface. No app can do this part for you — including ours.
