Crypto behaves differently from almost everything else your family might inherit. A bank knows you have an account with them. A pension provider has your details on file. Crypto doesn't work that way — there's no company that automatically knows you hold it, and no provider who will get in touch with your family when you die. If nobody knows it's there, or nobody can reach the keys, it can be gone for good.

> *This is general information to help you get organized, not legal, tax, or financial advice. Rules on wills, probate and cryptoasset taxation vary by country and change over time — confirm specifics with a qualified solicitor or accountant.*

None of this is a reason to avoid holding crypto. It's a reason to write down what you have and where — the same instinct that already applies to a pension you haven't thought about in years, or a savings account nobody else knows the sort code for.

## Why crypto is different from a bank account when you die

When someone dies, an executor can write to their bank, building society or pension provider, prove who they are, and the provider deals with the rest. There's a process, a phone number, and — eventually — an answer.

Crypto has no equivalent central point of contact in most cases. Access to crypto is controlled entirely by whoever holds the private key or seed phrase for a wallet. There's no institution to write to, no customer number to quote, and no "forgotten account" process that finds it for you. If the keys are lost — because nobody knew to look for them, or the one person who did has died — the crypto isn't frozen or held in trust somewhere waiting for a claim. It's simply unreachable, permanently, by design. That's not a bug in how crypto works; it's the whole point of self-custody. It just means the safety net families take for granted with a bank account doesn't exist here.

Exchange accounts (Coinbase, Kraken and similar platforms) are the exception — they're companies, and most have their own bereavement or account-closure process, roughly similar in shape to a bank's. The gap is specifically around self-custody wallets and any crypto nobody else knows about.

## The three things your family actually needs

Strip away the technical detail and your family needs exactly three things, and only one of them is sensitive:

1. **To know the crypto exists**, and roughly how much there is. Not a precise, constantly-updated figure — just enough that it isn't invisible.
2. **To know where and how it's held** — an exchange account, a self-custody software wallet, a hardware wallet kept in a drawer or safe. This tells your executor which situation they're in and who, if anyone, they need to contact.
3. **Secure access to the actual keys or recovery phrase** — kept completely separately from the first two, in a dedicated password manager or hardware backup with its own legacy-access setup. This is the one piece that should never sit in a will, a checklist, or anywhere your family's app or advisor can see it.

The first two are a findability problem. The third is a security problem. Treating them as the same thing is how crypto gets lost — either because the record was never written down, or because the keys were written down somewhere far too exposed.

## Crypto in your will (UK) — do's and don'ts

**Do** mention that you hold crypto as an asset, the same way you'd reference a savings account or an investment portfolio — this helps your executor understand the shape of the estate and know to look for it.

**Don't** write your private keys, seed phrase, or exchange passwords into your will, or into any document that gets filed alongside it. As covered in [should you put passwords in your will?](/blog/should-you-put-passwords-in-your-will), a will can become a public document once probate is granted in England and Wales, and it's slow to update — the opposite of what a set of keys needs.

**Do** consider naming an executor who's comfortable with the basics of crypto, or briefing your existing executor on what self-custody means and why it's different from a bank account — a well-meaning executor who's never heard of a seed phrase can waste weeks not realizing what they're looking for.

> *Wills and probate rules vary between England & Wales, Scotland and Northern Ireland, and by country elsewhere. Get your will reviewed by a qualified solicitor.*

## Does inheritance tax apply to crypto? (UK)

In general terms, yes — HMRC treats cryptoassets as property, so they're counted as part of your estate for inheritance tax purposes, valued in sterling at the date of death, the same as shares or a savings account would be. Whether any tax is actually due depends on the size of the whole estate, available allowances and reliefs, and rules that can change over time. For the mechanics of thresholds and rates generally, see [how much is inheritance tax? UK thresholds and rates explained](/blog/how-much-is-inheritance-tax).

This isn't tax advice, and crypto valuations can be more volatile than most other assets — talk to an accountant or tax adviser who can look at the estate as a whole.

## A simple plan to make your crypto findable (not lost)

The fix isn't complicated, but it does mean keeping two things deliberately separate:

1. **A record of what you own and where.** A written inventory — which platforms, which wallets, roughly what they're worth — that your executor or a trusted person can actually find. This is the part most people skip, because it feels like it can wait.
2. **The keys, kept somewhere secure and separate.** A reputable password manager with an emergency or legacy-access feature, or a hardware backup, with its own plan for how a trusted person could reach it if they truly needed to.

Write the first one down properly, and get comfortable that the second is somewhere safe. Together, that's the difference between crypto your family can act on and crypto that quietly disappears.

## Frequently asked questions

**Can I put my seed phrase or private keys in my will?**
No. In England and Wales a will can become a public document once probate is granted, so anything written into it may eventually be readable by strangers — the exact opposite of what a seed phrase needs. A will is also slow to change, while crypto holdings and wallets can change often. Keep the keys themselves in a dedicated password manager or hardware backup with its own legacy-access setup, and use the will (or a letter alongside it) only to point to the fact that they exist — never to the keys themselves.

**What's the difference between an exchange account and a self-custody wallet, for this purpose?**
It changes who your family has to deal with. An exchange account (Coinbase, Binance, Kraken and similar) is held with a company that has its own bereavement process — broadly similar in shape to a bank, usually needing a death certificate and a grant of probate. A self-custody wallet has no company in the loop at all: whoever holds the private key or seed phrase controls the crypto, full stop. There's no customer service line to call and no dispute process if the keys are lost. Record which kind you hold and roughly where, so your family knows which situation they're in before they start.

**Will my executor have to pay tax on my crypto?**
HMRC treats cryptoassets as property, so they form part of the estate for inheritance tax purposes like any other asset, valued in GBP at the date of death. Whether tax is actually due depends on the size of the whole estate, reliefs and allowances, and rules can change — this isn't tax advice, so get the specifics confirmed by a solicitor or accountant who can look at the full estate.

**What actually happens if no one knows my crypto exists?**
Nothing happens — that's the risk. There's no central register of who owns what crypto, and no provider who will proactively contact your family the way a bank sometimes does. If nobody knows an exchange account or wallet exists, it simply isn't found, and if nobody has the keys to a self-custody wallet, the crypto is permanently inaccessible even once it's found. Unlike a lost bank account, there's no dormant-asset scheme or tracing service that can recover it later.

## The short version

Crypto is easy to lose on death because there's no provider who automatically knows it exists and no process that finds it for you. Reference it as an asset in your will — never the keys. Keep a findable record of what you own and where it's held, keep the keys themselves somewhere secure and completely separate, and make sure one person you trust knows both pieces exist.
