Most of us could not, off the top of our head, list everything we own. We carry a rough sense of it — the house, the cars, "some savings," a drawer of paperwork somewhere — but not an actual list, with values, and a note of where each record lives. That gap is quietly expensive. It shows up when you file an insurance claim, work out your net worth, sort out a parent's affairs, or simply try to feel organised and can't.

A written **personal asset inventory** closes the gap in about an hour. This guide walks you through it the completeness-first way: a categorized checklist so nothing gets missed, a step-by-step method, a free template you can copy, and the one habit that keeps the list from going stale. If you want the wider family framing first, start with [what a family asset inventory is](/blog/what-is-a-family-asset-inventory/); this post is the hands-on "how to build the list."

It's written UK-first, but the method travels — the categories and steps apply anywhere, even where the specific tax or legal terms differ.

## Why an hour on this is worth it

A list of what you own isn't busywork. It pays off in four concrete ways:

- **Organisation.** One place answers "what do we have, and where's the proof?" — instead of four apps, three drawers, and one person's memory.
- **Insurance.** If you had a fire or a burglary, a valued list is the difference between a smooth claim and reconstructing a household from memory under stress.
- **Net worth.** A complete list of what you own and owe is the raw material for [calculating your net worth](/blog/how-to-calculate-your-familys-net-worth/) — you can't total what you haven't listed.
- **Peace of mind.** If something happened to you, your family wouldn't be left guessing what exists or where to find it.

You only have to build it once. After that it's five minutes of upkeep now and then.

## What to include: the complete checklist

Completeness is the whole point, so work through categories rather than trusting memory. Here are the seven buckets that, between them, catch almost everything a person owns:

- **Property and land** — the place you live, plus any second home, rental, holiday place, or plot of land you hold a share in. List each one now; you can attach a rough value later.
- **Vehicles** — anything with a logbook or title: the family cars, a motorbike, a caravan, a boat, even a ride-on mower. Note each by make and rough year so it's identifiable.
- **Financial accounts** — every current and savings account, ISA or retirement account, pension, and investment holding. One line each, named by the provider, so you can find it again when it matters.
- **Valuables and contents** — the things that would hurt to lose and cost real money to replace: jewellery, watches, art, instruments, high-end tech, collections. Walking room by room catches the ones you'd never recall from a chair.
- **Digital assets** — value that lives online: domain names, a monetised creator or social account, loyalty-point and airline-mile balances, cryptocurrency, and paid software or game licences.
- **Important documents** — not assets themselves, but the paper that proves them: property deeds, your will, insurance policies, passports, share certificates. Record where each one physically lives.
- **What you owe** — the mortgage, car finance, credit-card balances, student or personal loans. Listing debts alongside assets turns a pile of possessions into a true net position.

Same seven buckets suit almost every household; the contents just differ. If a category is empty for you, tick it off and move on — the value of the checklist is confirming you *considered* it.

## Step by step: build it so nothing gets missed

The reason inventories fail isn't difficulty, it's gaps — you list the obvious things and forget the account you never log into or the ring in the safe. Two passes fix that, because each one surfaces what the other misses.

**1. Set up your columns first.** Decide where the list will live (see the template below) before you start, so you're capturing into structure, not a wall of notes.

**2. Do an account-by-account pass.** Open your banking app, your email, and your document drawer, and log every provider you see: accounts, pensions, investments, insurance policies, subscriptions. This sweep is what catches the *invisible* assets — the dormant savings account, the old workplace pension, the policy you set up years ago.

**3. Do a room-by-room pass.** Now walk the house. Go room to room and log the physical, valuable, and proof-bearing things: electronics, jewellery, tools, the art on the wall, the documents in the filing cabinet. Photograph as you go — a photo is faster than a sentence and doubles as evidence.

**4. Add what you owe.** Finish with debts, so the list reflects your real position, not just the shiny side.

**5. Leave gaps rather than skipping.** Don't stall on a value you're unsure of. Enter the item, mark the value "to confirm," and move on. An entry with a blank still counts; a forgotten item doesn't.

Doing the two passes in one sitting is what makes the list *complete* — the account sweep and the physical walk rarely turn up the same things.

## The free template

You don't need special software to start — a simple table does the job. Copy these five columns into a spreadsheet, a note, or a printed sheet:

| Item | Category | Details | Approx. value | Where the record lives |
|------|----------|---------|---------------|------------------------|
| Home | Property | 3-bed, purchased 2019 | To confirm | Deeds — filing cabinet |
| Main car | Vehicle | [BrandX], 2021 reg | £14,000 | V5C — filing cabinet |
| Cash ISA | Financial account | [Provider], opened 2020 | £8,500 | [Provider] app |
| Wedding ring | Valuable | 18ct gold, appraised 2022 | £2,200 | Valuation cert — safe |
| Domain name | Digital asset | example.com, auto-renews | £Nominal | [Registrar] account |
| Mortgage | What you owe | [Lender], 22 yrs left | −£210,000 | [Lender] portal |

A few notes on the columns:

- **Category** is what lets you sort and spot gaps — group the finished list and it's obvious if a whole bucket is empty.
- **Approx. value** can be a range or "to confirm." A rough number beats a blank; a blank beats nothing at all.
- **Where the record lives** is the column people skip and later wish they hadn't. It's the difference between a list of *things* and a list that actually leads you to the deeds, the policy, or the login.

That final column is where a plain spreadsheet starts to strain: it can name *where* a record lives, but it can't hold the record. The photo of the receipt, the scan of the valuation, the PDF policy — those end up somewhere else, and the link between item and proof breaks.

## Start with one category

The complete list can feel like a lot, so don't try to do it all at once. Pick the category that would cause the most trouble if it went missing — usually financial accounts or valuables — and list just that today. Momentum does the rest. A partial, accurate list you keep is far more useful than a perfect one you never finish.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is a personal asset inventory?**
It's one organized list of everything you own — property, vehicles, financial accounts, valuables, digital assets, and important documents — usually alongside what you owe, so it shows your true position. Unlike a quick jotting on a notepad, a proper inventory records a few useful details per item (a category, rough value, and where the record or proof lives) so it's actually usable for insurance, organisation, net worth, or leaving your family a clear picture.

**What should a personal asset inventory include?**
Work through seven buckets so nothing slips: property and land; vehicles; financial accounts and investments; valuables and household contents; digital assets; important documents; and what you owe. For each item, capture the category, a rough value or value-range, and where the record lives (which bank, which drawer, which app). You don't need every field filled on day one — a category and a location beat a blank line.

**How do I make sure I don't miss anything?**
Use two passes that catch different things. First go account-by-account: open your banking app and email and list every provider you see, which surfaces accounts and policies. Then go room-by-room through the house, which surfaces physical valuables and documents you'd never remember from a chair. Photograph items and receipts as you go so capture is fast, and leave the value blank rather than skipping an item — an entry with a gap still counts; a missing entry doesn't.

**What is the best way to keep a personal asset inventory?**
The format matters less than whether you'll keep it current. A spreadsheet works if you're disciplined; a purpose-built vault reduces the friction that makes most lists go stale, because you can photograph an item and have a record drafted from it rather than typing every field. Whatever you choose, keep it in one private place, back it up, and store it separately from the items themselves so a fire or theft can't take the list along with the belongings.
