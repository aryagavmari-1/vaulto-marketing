**No — don't put passwords in your will.** Two reasons, both practical. In England and Wales a will can become a public document once probate is granted, so anything written into it may end up readable by strangers. And a will is deliberately hard to change, while passwords change constantly — so they'd be out of date long before anyone read them.

> *This is general information to help you get organized, not legal, tax, or financial advice. Rules on wills and probate vary by country (and between England & Wales, Scotland, and Northern Ireland) and change over time — confirm specifics with a qualified solicitor.*

That leaves the real question, which is the one most people are actually asking: **if not the will, then where?** The useful answer starts with separating two things that get muddled together.

## The two different things people are trying to solve

When someone asks "how do I leave my account details for my family," they're usually holding two separate problems in one sentence:

1. **Credentials** — the passwords, PINs and two-factor codes that unlock things. These are sensitive, they rotate, and there are tools built specifically to hold them.
2. **The map** — what accounts, policies, property and paperwork actually *exist*, and where the proof of each one lives. This is the part almost nobody writes down.

They need completely different homes, and conflating them is why people end up doing something risky (a password list in a drawer) or nothing at all.

Here's the part worth sitting with: **the map matters more than the credentials.** A family with your logins but no idea the 2011 pension exists will never find it. A family with a clear list of what exists — but no logins — can contact every provider directly and go through the proper process for each one. The list is what turns months of detective work into a series of phone calls.

## Why a will is the wrong place for either

**A will can become public.** In England and Wales, once probate is granted, the will forms part of the probate record. Anyone can search for it and order a copy through the [GOV.UK "Find a will" service](https://www.gov.uk/search-will-probate) for a small fee. This is intentional — it lets beneficiaries and creditors see how an estate is being administered — but it means a will is not a private container. Scotland and Northern Ireland operate separate systems, and the position differs again in other countries.

**A will doesn't move at the speed of a password.** Changing a will properly means a codicil or a new will, executed with witnesses. You are not going to do that because you rotated your email password. Anything time-sensitive written into a will is stale almost immediately.

**A will is read at the wrong moment.** It comes into play after death, often weeks later, once probate is underway. Practical access questions — a bill that needs paying, a policy that needs claiming — tend to arrive far sooner.

So the will does the job it's good at: who gets what, who administers it. Access details belong elsewhere. (If you're earlier in the process, the [will planning checklist](/blog/will-planning-checklist/) covers what to gather before you write one.)

## Where credentials should actually live

There are two reasonable options, and both have honest limitations.

**Option 1: a reputable password manager with emergency or legacy access.** This is the mainstream answer, and it's a good one. A password manager holds all your logins behind one master password, generates strong unique passwords, and — this is the part that matters here — most established ones offer some form of emergency or legacy access: you nominate a trusted person who can request access, with a waiting period before it's granted. We take no position on which manager to use and have no affiliate arrangement; the category is what matters, not the brand.

*The honest limitations:* it only works if you actually configure the emergency access feature — installing the app isn't enough. Your nominated person needs to know they've been nominated and roughly what to do. And feature names and rules differ between providers, so read how yours actually works rather than assuming.

**Option 2: a sealed written record, stored *with* your will rather than *in* it.** Some people prefer paper, and for a small number of critical items that's defensible. The key distinction is that the record sits alongside the will — with your solicitor, in a safe, wherever your will is kept — rather than forming part of the document that becomes public.

*The honest limitations:* paper goes stale, and nothing reminds you to update it. It can be lost, damaged, or found by the wrong person. And whether your will can properly reference a separate sealed record — and how to word that so it doesn't cause problems — is genuinely a solicitor question, not something to improvise from a blog post.

Whichever you choose, three rules travel with it: keep it current, tell exactly one or two trusted people it exists and how to reach it, and never put the contents into any document that gets filed with the will.

## A short list of what not to do

- **Don't email yourself a password list.** Your email account is the reset route for everything else, which makes it the worst place to keep the keys to everything else.
- **Don't keep a phone note called "Passwords."** A lost or borrowed phone hands over the lot, and it's the first place anyone would look.
- **Don't write PINs on or near the cards they belong to.** Same drawer counts as near.
- **Don't rely on one person's memory.** In most households one person knows where everything is. The plan has to work when that person is the one who's unavailable.
- **Don't write credentials into the will, or any document filed with it.** That's the whole point of this piece.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is my will a public document?**
In England and Wales, yes — once probate has been granted. At that point the will becomes part of the probate record, and anyone can search for it and order a copy through the GOV.UK "Find a will" service for a small fee. That isn't a loophole; it's how the system is designed to work, so beneficiaries and creditors can see the basis on which an estate is being administered. Before probate is granted, a will is private. Scotland and Northern Ireland run separate systems with their own rules, and practice varies in other countries — so confirm the position where you live with a solicitor. The practical consequence is the same everywhere it applies: treat anything written in a will as something that may eventually be read by people you never chose.

**Can I leave my passwords in a letter of wishes instead?**
A letter of wishes is more private than a will — it usually isn't filed with the probate record — but it's still the wrong container for credentials. It's a paper document that goes stale the moment you change a password, it can be copied or mislaid, and it may be stored somewhere your family can't reach quickly. The better pattern is to keep the credentials themselves in a reputable password manager with an emergency or legacy access feature, and use the letter to record that the password manager exists, which one it is, and who should be able to reach it — a pointer, not the contents. Whether and how to reference that alongside your will is worth asking your solicitor about.

**What happens to online accounts when someone dies?**
Each provider decides, and the answers differ a lot. Many large platforms have a documented bereavement or legacy process: a nominated contact, a memorialisation option, or a route for an executor to request closure or a data copy with a death certificate. Banks, pension providers and insurers have their own notification processes and will usually freeze an account once told. Subscriptions often just keep charging until someone cancels them. The practical implication is that your family needs to know which accounts exist far more than they need your logins — with a list of what exists, they can go through each provider's proper process. Without one, they're guessing.

**Can my executor legally use my logins?**
Usually not, even with the best intentions. An online account is generally governed by the terms you agreed to, and those terms typically say the account is personal to you and not transferable — so logging in as you can breach them even where the executor has clear authority over the estate itself. The recognised route is to deal with each provider directly, using their bereavement or legacy process and the grant of probate where required. This is general information rather than legal advice, and the detail varies by provider and jurisdiction, so check with a solicitor if an account matters materially to the estate.
