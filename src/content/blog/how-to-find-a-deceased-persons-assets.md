When a parent or partner dies, one of the hardest early jobs is also the most practical: working out what they actually owned, what they owed, and where the records are. If you've been named executor — or you're simply the family member who has stepped up — you may be starting almost from scratch, with a drawer of paperwork and a lot of unknowns.

This is a step-by-step checklist for finding a deceased person's assets and getting them organized. It's written UK-first, but the approach travels: work through where to look, how to check for a will, and how to keep track of everything you uncover so nothing slips through the cracks. By the end you'll have a method for turning scattered clues into a single, trustworthy inventory of the estate.

## First, set your expectation: this is a search, not a lookup

There is no master list of everything a person owned. Finding a deceased person's assets means following a paper trail — post, statements, tax letters, and the accounts those documents point to — and using tracing services for anything that's gone quiet. It takes patience, and things will surface weeks apart. The goal of this guide is to make that search systematic, so you can be confident you've looked in the right places.

Keep one running record as you go (more on that below). Every asset and debt you confirm belongs in the same place, because you'll eventually need a full valuation of the estate to apply for probate and settle any inheritance tax.

## Where to look: a room-by-room search checklist

Work through these categories in rough passes rather than trying to be exhaustive in one sitting. Tick off what you find, and note where each original document lives.

### Paperwork, post, and email

The single richest source is the person's own paperwork. Redirect their post if you can, and don't throw anything away until the estate is settled.

- **Bank and building-society statements**, cheque books, and paying-in books
- **Tax letters** from HMRC (or your country's tax authority) — these often name income sources, pensions, and accounts
- **Insurance documents** — life, home, car, private medical
- **Share certificates** and dividend vouchers
- **Their email inbox**, where reachable and legally appropriate, for online-only statements and renewal notices

Our checklist of [important documents every family should keep](/blog/important-family-documents-to-keep/) doubles as a list of what to hunt for now.

### Property and physical assets

- **Property** — title deeds or a Land Registry search will confirm ownership and any mortgage
- **Vehicles** — the V5C logbook confirms a car, van, or motorbike
- **Valuables** — jewellery, art, collections, and anything with a separate insurance valuation

A structured [family asset inventory](/blog/what-is-a-family-asset-inventory/) is a useful model for what to capture about each item, whether you're doing this for the estate or for your own household.

### Bank and building-society accounts

Statements and post point to most accounts. For anything you suspect but can't locate — a childhood building-society passbook, an old savings account — the UK's free **My Lost Account** service searches banks, building societies, and NS&I on your behalf.

### Pensions and life insurance

Pensions are among the most commonly missed assets. Check payslips, P60s, and old employment records for scheme names, then use the government's free **Pension Tracing Service** to find schemes you can't track down. Life insurance and death-in-service benefits are easy to overlook — look for policy documents, and ask former employers.

### Investments, premium bonds, and shares

- **NS&I Premium Bonds** and savings certificates — NS&I can confirm holdings and any unclaimed prizes
- **Shares and funds** — dividend vouchers and letters from registrars such as Computershare or Equiniti point to holdings; registrars can also trace unclaimed dividends
- **Investment and stocks-and-shares ISAs** — look for annual statements

### Digital accounts and crypto

Modern estates increasingly live online. Look for a password manager, and check for PayPal and other payment wallets, online-only banks, and any cryptocurrency (an exchange account or a hardware wallet). Our [digital estate-planning checklist](/blog/digital-estate-planning-checklist/) explains the estate side of digital accounts in more depth.

### Business interests

If the person ran or part-owned a business, check **Companies House** for directorships and shareholdings, plus any partnership agreements, business bank accounts, and accountant's records.

### Debts and liabilities

An estate is what's owned *minus* what's owed, so debts matter as much as assets. Look for the mortgage, loans, credit and store cards, hire-purchase agreements, and outstanding utility or council-tax bills. The estate's **credit report** is one of the best ways to surface both accounts and debts held in the person's name.

### Unclaimed and forgotten assets

For anything that seems to have vanished, tracing services are your friend: **My Lost Account** (dormant accounts), the **Pension Tracing Service** (lost pensions), and the commercial **Unclaimed Assets Register** (which searches further sources for a fee). *Rules vary by country* — if the person held money abroad, check the equivalent services there.

## How to check for a will

Before you get too far, confirm whether there's a will — it names who's entitled to what and who should be administering the estate. Look in the obvious places (a home safe, a filing cabinet, a memory box), then check with their solicitor, their bank, and the national will register if one exists. Many people also leave a "where everything is" letter alongside the will.

## Frequently asked questions

**How do I find bank accounts I don't know about after someone dies?**
Start with the paper trail: unopened post, bank and building-society statements, cheque books, and old tax letters often reveal accounts nobody remembered. The estate's credit report is useful too — it lists accounts and debts held in the person's name. For accounts that seem lost, the UK's free My Lost Account service searches banks, building societies and NS&I. There is no single database that lists every account someone held, so treat it as a search you work through, not a one-click lookup.

**Is there a way to find unclaimed assets of the deceased?**
Yes, several free and low-cost UK tracing routes exist. My Lost Account covers dormant bank, building-society and NS&I accounts; the government's Pension Tracing Service helps locate lost workplace and personal pensions; and share registrars such as Computershare or Equiniti can trace unclaimed dividends and shareholdings. The commercial Unclaimed Assets Register searches further sources for a fee. Rules and services vary by country, so check the equivalents where the person lived and held money.

**How do I find a deceased person's pensions?**
Check payslips, P60s and old employment records for scheme names, then look for annual statements from providers. For anything you can't trace, the government's free Pension Tracing Service can find contact details for workplace and personal pension schemes from an employer or provider name. Contact each scheme directly to confirm what's held and how it's paid out.

**Do I need to find every asset before applying for probate?**
You need a reasonable valuation of the estate — everything owned and owed — to apply for a grant of probate and to work out any inheritance tax, so a thorough search matters. You don't have to be perfect on the first pass; keep a running inventory and update it as more comes to light. A probate solicitor or the relevant tax authority can tell you what's required for your situation.

**Can an app find a deceased person's accounts automatically?**
No. No consumer app can log into or aggregate another person's financial accounts without their credentials, and none can "discover" unknown accounts for you — finding assets after a death is a manual search through paperwork, post and tracing services.
