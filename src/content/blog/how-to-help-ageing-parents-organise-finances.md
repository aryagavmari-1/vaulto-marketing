# How to Help Ageing Parents Get Their Finances Organised

Most families don't plan this conversation — it happens by accident. A hospital form asks
for an insurance policy number nobody can find. A sibling asks "wait, does Mum even have a
will?" and the room goes quiet. It's rarely a crisis that starts the process. More often
it's a small moment that reveals how little anyone actually knows.

The good news: if your parent is still healthy and capable, you have the easiest version of
this task available to you. This is about getting organised together, now, while it's simple
— not about taking anything over.

## Why this gets harder if you wait

Right now, your parent can answer questions. They know which bank they use, where the
paperwork for the house is, who their financial adviser is, and what that second insurance
policy is actually for. Every one of those facts lives, at least in part, in their head.

That's not a problem today. It becomes one if it's ever reconstructed after the fact —
guessed at from a filing cabinet, pieced together from old statements, or asked of a bank
that won't tell anyone anything without the right paperwork. Sorting it out later is possible,
but it's slower, more stressful, and it happens at the worst possible time.

None of this is about assuming the worst. It's simply easier to write things down while the
person who knows them can still do the explaining. Think of it the way you'd think about a
handover at work: better to walk through it together than to leave someone guessing.

## Start with a conversation, not a spreadsheet

The instinct is to open a spreadsheet and start asking for account numbers. Resist it. The
first step isn't data collection — it's a conversation, and how you open it matters more than
what you ask.

A few things that tend to help:

- **Lead with your own situation.** "I've been getting my own finances organised and it made
  me realise I have no idea what you've got in place — can we go through yours together?"
  lands very differently from "you need to tell me your finances."
- **Name the actual worry.** Most parents aren't afraid of the conversation itself — they're
  afraid of losing control. Say plainly that this isn't about taking over anything; it's about
  making sure the information exists somewhere both of you can find it.
- **Let them set the pace.** One conversation rarely covers everything, and it doesn't need
  to. A first pass that covers "what exists" is a good outcome. Values, balances and details
  can follow later.
- **Expect it to take more than one sitting.** Treat it as an ongoing, low-key project rather
  than a single difficult afternoon.

Your parent stays the one who knows and controls their own finances throughout. Your job is
to help make what they already know easier to find — not to manage it for them.

## What to gather: the checklist

Once the conversation is underway, a simple checklist keeps things concrete without turning it
into an interrogation. You don't need every line filled in on day one — the point is knowing
what exists and roughly where, so nothing important is a total blank later.

- **Accounts** — current and savings accounts, and which bank(s) they're with
- **Property** — the home, any other property, and where the deeds or mortgage paperwork sit
- **Pensions** — state pension and any workplace or private pensions
- **Insurance** — life, home, health and any other policies, with provider names
- **Key documents** — will, ID documents, property paperwork, tax records
- **Important contacts** — financial adviser, solicitor, accountant, insurance broker

For a fuller rundown of exactly what counts as an "important document" and how long to keep
it, see our guide on [important family documents to keep](/blog/important-family-documents-to-keep/).

## Put it in one place both of you can see

Scattered notes, a shared login, or a manila folder that only your parent knows the location
of all have the same weakness: the information only really exists in one head, or one drawer.
A single shared record — kept up to date as things change — beats all three.

(helping capture items, reviewing what's been added, or just seeing progress). It isn't an

a way of accessing the accounts themselves. You're cataloguing the picture, not taking over
the accounts.

## Keep your parent in control — and keep it private

It's worth saying this out loud early, because it's usually the real hesitation under the
surface: helping organise the finances is not the same as taking them over.

A few things worth being explicit about with your parent:

- **They stay in control.** It's their vault; you're invited in for a specific purpose, with a
  role, and that invitation can be ended whenever they choose.
- **You don't need their passwords to help.** Because the vault records *what exists*, not
  login credentials, your parent never has to hand over the passwords to their actual bank or
  investment accounts for this to work.
- **It's a private vault, not a shared inbox.** Our support team cannot browse your parent's
  vault, and there's no staff account type that could be given access to it.

Being upfront about these points tends to make the whole project easier — a lot of the
resistance to "let's get this organised" comes from an unspoken worry about losing control,
and naming it directly usually clears it.

## Keep it current

A finance record that's accurate once and stale forever isn't much better than no record at
all. The good news is that keeping it current doesn't need to be a big undertaking — a light
annual check-in is usually enough, plus a quick update after any obvious life event: a new
account, a house move, a policy that's changed, or a pension that's been consolidated.

Treat it the way you'd treat any other household admin — a once-a-year "does anything need
updating" conversation, rather than a project you finish and forget.

## When more is needed

Organising *what exists* is the proactive, while-everyone's-well version of this work — and
for most families, most of the time, it's the right place to start.

cover what happens if your parent is ever unable to manage their own affairs. That's a
different conversation with different paperwork, and it's worth having once the basics are in
