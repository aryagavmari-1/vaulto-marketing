If a ring or a watch was stolen tomorrow, could you tell your insurer exactly what it was, what it was worth, and prove you owned it? For most of us the honest answer is no — we know roughly what we own, but not in a form an insurer can act on.

This guide focuses on jewellery, watches, art, and other high-value items — the pieces that need more than a line in a general contents list. If you haven't inventoried the rest of your home yet, start with [how to make a home inventory](/blog/how-to-make-a-home-inventory/) or the broader [home contents inventory for insurance](/blog/home-contents-inventory-for-insurance/); this post picks up where those leave off.

## Why valuables need their own record

Standard contents insurance is built for the *average* household item, not the exceptional one. Most policies cap what they'll pay out on any single item — often a few hundred pounds — unless it's been "scheduled" or "specified" separately on the policy. A single ring or watch can easily exceed that cap on its own, which means it can sit *inside* your contents cover on paper but *outside* it in practice.

Specifying an item usually requires two things you can't produce after a loss: a documented value, and proof it was yours. Both need to exist *before* anything happens — this guide is the practical fix.

## What to capture per item

A rough description in your head isn't evidence. For each piece, aim to capture:

- **A clear description** — material, stones, maker or brand, and any distinguishing detail ("gold ring with sapphire" is weak; "18ct gold, 1.2ct sapphire, Cartier, size M" is strong).
- **Photos, including any hallmark or serial number** — jewellery hallmarks and watch serials are some of the strongest proof of identity an insurer or the police can use.
- **Purchase record or receipt**, if you have one.
- **A professional appraisal or valuation, and its date** — this is what most insurers actually rely on for higher-value pieces.
- **Where it's kept** — a home safe, a bank deposit box, worn daily. Some insurers ask, since it affects risk.

## Getting valuations right

A valuation is the number your cover, and your eventual payout, is built on — so it's worth getting right, and worth being honest about who should produce it.

For anything genuinely valuable — an engagement ring, a family heirloom, a serious watch, a piece of art — get it appraised by a qualified, independent valuer or jeweller, not estimated from memory. **Vaulto can suggest a rough value range for items like these, but that's a working estimate, not the independent appraisal your insurer will expect** — treat it as a starting point, and use Vaulto to record and keep the number a professional gives you, alongside the date they gave it.

That date matters. Gold and precious-metal prices shift, watch markets move, and a valuation from five years ago can be meaningfully out of date. As a starting point, plan to have higher-value pieces revalued every two to three years, and sooner for anything tracking a volatile material.

## Photographing valuables so a claim actually pays

Photos do a lot of the evidentiary work, and a few habits make them far more useful:

- **Shoot the whole piece**, then a **close-up with something for scale** — a ruler or a coin next to it works well.
- **Get the hallmark, engraving, or serial number** in sharp focus — this is often the single most useful shot you take.
- **Use good, even light** and a plain background; a cluttered shot makes details hard to verify later.
- **Photograph sets together as well as individually** — a pair of earrings or a set of rings should have one photo showing the group and one showing each piece, so nothing gets confused for something else.

Ten minutes with a phone camera, done properly, is worth more at claim time than a vague memory of "the gold one with the blue stone."

## Where to store the record

Here's a gap that catches people out: they gather receipts, appraisals, and photos — then keep it all in the same drawer or safe as the jewellery itself. If that safe is lost to a burglary or a fire, the evidence disappears with the items it was meant to prove you owned.

The record needs to live somewhere separate from the valuables — durable, off-site, reachable even when the physical items can't be. A private digital record does this well, provided it's actually private rather than a shared spreadsheet or a folder synced to every device in the house.

## Keep it current

An inventory that isn't updated quietly becomes wrong. Add a new piece the week you buy, inherit, or have something reset or re-appraised — while the receipt and the memory are both fresh. Then do a full review once a year, ideally timed to your policy renewal, so your single-item limits and valuations stay matched to reality rather than to what was true a few years ago.

## Quick starter checklist

For each valuable item, capture:

1. A clear photo of the whole piece
2. A close-up of any hallmark, engraving, or serial number, with something for scale
3. A written description (material, stones, maker, size)
4. The purchase receipt, if you have one
5. A professional appraisal or valuation, and its date
6. Where it's kept
7. Whether it needs to be specified separately on your policy

Do the five or six most valuable pieces in your home first — that's usually where most of the exposure sits — then work through the rest as you have time.

## Frequently asked questions

**How often should I get my jewellery and valuables revalued?**
As a rule of thumb, every two to three years for most pieces — and sooner for anything where the underlying material moves in price, like gold, platinum, or diamonds, where a valuation can drift meaningfully in a single year. Review it whenever your policy renews, and always after buying, inheriting, or having a piece altered or reset. An out-of-date valuation is one of the most common reasons a payout falls short of replacement cost.

**What if I don't have the original receipt for a piece?**
A receipt helps but isn't the only proof insurers accept. Photos that clearly show hallmarks or engraved serial numbers, a professional valuation or appraisal, the original box or certificate, or even bank/card statements from the purchase can all support a claim. Inherited pieces are the hardest case — a current appraisal is usually the best evidence you can create going forward, so it's worth getting one even without a purchase history.

**Does standard home insurance cover my jewellery, or do I need to list it separately?**
Most standard contents policies include jewellery only up to a single-item limit — often lower than people expect — so anything above that needs to be "scheduled" or "specified" on the policy individually, usually backed by a valuation. Check your policy's single-item cap against what each of your valuable pieces is actually worth; it's the easiest gap to miss and one of the easiest to fix.

**What's the best way to photograph jewellery for an insurance record?**
Take a few shots per piece: one showing the whole item at normal size, one close-up with something for scale (a ruler or a coin), and one macro shot of any hallmark, engraving, or serial number. Shoot in good natural light against a plain background, and if you own several similar pieces — a set of rings, say — photograph them together as well as individually so an adjuster can tell them apart.

**Should I keep the paperwork for my valuables in the same place as the items?**
No — that's one of the most common gaps. If a safe or a drawer is lost to a theft or a fire, the receipts and appraisals stored right next to the items go with them, and you're left trying to prove a loss with nothing to prove it with. Keep the record — photos, receipts, valuations — somewhere separate from the physical pieces, ideally a private digital record you can still reach even if the house or the safe can't be.
