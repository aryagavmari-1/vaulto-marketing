Someone has died, and somewhere on the list of things to sort out is "the bank account." It's rarely the first call anyone wants to make, and it's not always obvious what to expect — whether the account is instantly frozen, whether probate is needed to touch a penny, or what happens to the direct debits still ticking along in the background.

This is a plain-English walkthrough of what actually happens to a UK bank account after a death: who can access it, when, and what the bank will ask for. It's written UK-first, because the rules — and the vocabulary — are specific to England, Wales, Scotland and Northern Ireland.

> *This is general information to help you understand the process, not legal, tax, or financial advice. Bank policies and probate rules vary and change over time — confirm specifics with the bank itself or a qualified solicitor.*

## The account gets frozen as soon as the bank is told

The moment a bank or building society learns that an account holder has died, it freezes the account. Standing orders, direct debits, and card payments stop; no one — not even someone with a card and PIN — can withdraw or spend from it. This isn't the bank being unhelpful; it's there to protect the estate, so the money is preserved until it's clear who's entitled to it and what's owed.

Interest usually keeps accruing on savings while the account is frozen, and any pending or scheduled payments are typically returned or cancelled, so it's worth telling anyone the deceased paid regularly — a landlord, a subscription, a utility company — separately, rather than assuming the bank will handle it for you.

## Sole accounts vs. joint accounts: two very different paths

What happens next depends heavily on how the account was held.

**A sole account** — in the deceased's name only — becomes part of their estate. It stays frozen until whoever is dealing with the estate (the executor, if there's a will, or an administrator if there isn't) can show the bank they're entitled to instruct it. For larger balances, that usually means producing a grant of probate or letters of administration. Below a threshold each bank sets for itself — commonly somewhere in the low thousands up to around £50,000, though it varies — many banks will release the funds without waiting for a grant, against a death certificate and the executor's ID.

**A joint account**, most commonly held by couples, is usually structured as "joint tenants" — meaning each holder owns the whole account, not a fixed half. On the first death, the balance passes automatically to the survivor by right of survivorship, outside the probate process entirely. The bank typically just needs sight of a certified death certificate to remove the deceased's name. One important catch: passing outside probate doesn't mean it's ignored for tax — the value still has to be reported when working out inheritance tax on the estate, since money one partner contributed can still count.

## What the bank will ask you for

Every institution is slightly different, but the shortlist is fairly consistent:

- A **certified copy of the death certificate** — most banks won't accept a photocopy, so it's worth ordering several from the registrar (ten is not excessive if there are several banks, a pension provider, and a property involved).
- **Proof of your own identity** and your relationship to the deceased — executor, next of kin, or the person dealing with the estate.
- The **will**, if there is one, and eventually a **grant of probate** or **letters of administration** for anything above the bank's release threshold.
- **Account details** — sort code, account number, or at least the branch, if you have them, though a bank can usually trace an account from the deceased's name and address alone.

The **Death Notification Service** (a shared service run by the banking industry, separate from GOV.UK) lets you report a death to multiple member banks and building societies through one online form, rather than making the same call to each one individually. It's worth checking whether the relevant banks are members before you start ringing round. This is different from **Tell Us Once**, the government service offered when you register a death, which notifies departments like HMRC, the DWP, and the passport and driving licence agencies — not banks.

## Can any money come out before probate?

Sometimes, yes. Two common exceptions:

- **Funeral costs.** Most banks will release funds directly to the funeral director, or reimburse whoever paid the invoice, on production of the bill and a death certificate — even before probate and even from an otherwise frozen sole account. Ask about this specifically when you first contact the bank.
- **Small-balance release.** As above, many banks will release modest sole-account balances without a grant, using their own internal threshold.

Everything else in a sole account waits for probate (or the bank's small-balance exception) before it moves.

## Debts, overdrafts, and standing orders

An estate is what's owned *minus* what's owed, and a bank account can carry both. An overdraft or a linked credit card balance becomes a debt of the estate, to be settled from estate assets before anything is distributed to beneficiaries — it does not automatically transfer to a next of kin or joint account holder (unless they were also a named borrower). Standing orders and direct debits set up from the account are typically cancelled once the bank is notified, so anything the deceased was paying for that still needs to continue — a subscription, a bill on a property, a policy premium — needs a new instruction set up from elsewhere, usually the estate account once probate is granted.

## ISAs get their own set of rules

An ISA stops accepting new contributions the day the holder dies, but its tax-free wrapper doesn't disappear immediately — it continues to shelter interest and growth for a further period while the estate is administered (up to three years, or until administration finishes, whichever comes first). If the deceased had a spouse or civil partner, that person also becomes entitled to an **Additional Permitted Subscription**: a one-off extra ISA allowance equal to the value of the deceased's ISA, on top of their own normal annual allowance. It's a separate allowance, not the specific money, so it can be paid in from any source.

## How long it actually takes

A joint account, or a small sole-account balance under a bank's release threshold, can be sorted within days of providing a death certificate. Anything that needs a grant of probate moves on probate's timetable, not the bank's — and probate itself commonly takes several months in the UK once you include gathering valuations, submitting any inheritance tax return, and waiting for the probate registry. It's frustrating, but it isn't the bank slowing things down; the grant is the legal proof of who's allowed to instruct the account, and no institution can bypass that for a meaningful sum.

## Frequently asked questions

### What happens to a joint bank account when one person dies?

In most cases it passes automatically to the surviving account holder, outside the probate process. UK joint accounts are usually held as 'joint tenants', which means the whole balance belongs to the survivor by right — the bank typically just needs a certified death certificate to remove the deceased's name and continue the account in the survivor's name alone. The balance still has to be reported for inheritance tax purposes, even though the account itself doesn't go through probate.

### Do you need probate to access a bank account after someone dies?

It depends on the balance and how the account was held. Joint accounts usually pass to the survivor without probate. For an account held in the deceased's sole name, each bank sets its own limit — commonly somewhere between a few thousand and around £50,000 — below which it may release the funds against a death certificate and some ID, without waiting for a grant of probate. Above that limit, the bank will normally require a grant of probate (or letters of administration if there's no will) before releasing anything.

### How do you tell a bank that someone has died?

Contact each bank or building society directly — by phone or in branch, most now also accept it online — and expect to provide a certified copy of the death certificate, proof of your own identity, and your relationship to the deceased (executor, next of kin, and so on). The UK's Death Notification Service lets you report a death to several member banks and building societies in one submission, which saves repeating the same call multiple times. It's a separate service from the government's Tell Us Once, which reports a death to departments like HMRC and the DWP rather than to banks.

### Can funeral costs be paid from a deceased person's bank account before probate?

Often, yes. Many UK banks will release funds directly to a funeral director, or reimburse whoever paid the bill, on production of the invoice and a death certificate — even before probate is granted and even if the account would otherwise be frozen. Policies vary by bank, so it's worth asking specifically about this when you first notify them, rather than assuming it isn't possible.

### What happens to a deceased person's ISA?

The ISA stops accepting new money on the date of death, but it keeps its tax-free status for a further period while the estate is administered (up to three years, or until administration completes, whichever is sooner) — so any interest or growth in that window stays tax-free. A surviving spouse or civil partner also gets an Additional Permitted Subscription, an extra ISA allowance equal to the value of the deceased's ISA, on top of their own normal annual allowance. It doesn't have to be the same money — just an amount up to that value, into their own ISA.

## The honest bottom line

A UK bank account doesn't vanish or transfer itself when someone dies — it freezes, and what happens next depends on how it was held and how much it holds. Joint accounts usually pass straight to the survivor; sole accounts wait for a death certificate and, above a threshold, a grant of probate. Funeral costs are often the one exception a frozen account will still pay out for. None of it moves faster than the paperwork behind it — which is exactly why the accounts worth having on a list are the ones nobody has to hunt for later.
