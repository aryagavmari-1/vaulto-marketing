Most households have a version of the same pile: a folder, a drawer, or a battered box holding the passport, the mortgage deed, the insurance policies, the will. Everyone means to sort it out "properly" one day. And most people are caught between two instincts that feel like they're in tension — keep the originals somewhere physically safe, or get everything into a digital place you can actually search and share. Both instincts are correct. Neither one, on its own, is enough.

This is an honest look at what can actually go wrong with each approach — home storage and online storage — and, more usefully, a checklist for judging whether "storing documents online" is safe in a specific, checkable way rather than a reassuring one. We'll also cover where Vaulto fits, including the parts we deliberately don't claim.

*This is general information, not legal or financial advice. What must legally stay on paper varies by country — check with a qualified professional for anything that matters.*

## The real risks of keeping documents only at home

Physical storage feels safe because you can see it and touch it. It has its own failure modes, and they're worth naming plainly.

- **Fire and flood.** A basic fireproof box protects paper for a limited time at a limited temperature — read the rating before you trust it with anything irreplaceable. A flood, a house fire that burns hot enough or long enough, or a break-in doesn't respect the box's rating.
- **One copy, one place.** If the will, the deed, and the passport all live in the same drawer, one bad afternoon can take all of them at once. Physical originals are, almost by definition, singular.
- **"Where did we put it."** The document that matters most is often needed at the worst possible time — a hospital admission, a sudden bereavement, a house move gone wrong — and it's exactly then that "it's somewhere in the study" isn't good enough. A family member who doesn't know a document exists, or doesn't know where the box or the key is, effectively doesn't have access to it.
- **No index.** Most families' important paperwork isn't actually in one place. Some is with a solicitor, some at the bank, some in a drawer, one certificate still at a parents' house. Nobody has a single list of what exists and where — so even "safe" storage doesn't help if nobody can find the map.

None of this means paper storage is wrong. It means a fireproof box is a good home for a small set of originals, and a poor substitute for an organised, findable system on its own.

## The real risks of storing documents online

The digital side has its own honest list, and a service that skips past it with a reassuring slogan is worth being suspicious of.

- **Breach.** Any online service can be compromised — that risk doesn't go away because a company says "trust us," and it isn't unique to any one provider. What matters is whether records and files are actually encrypted at rest, so that a breach of storage alone doesn't hand over readable data.
- **Access.** Encryption answers "what if someone breaks in from outside." It doesn't answer "who, inside the company, can look at my files." Those are different questions, and a trustworthy service should have a clear, specific answer to both — not just the first one.
- **Lock-in.** If a service doesn't let you get your data back out in a usable form, you're not really storing your documents there — you're renting access to them, on terms that can change. That risk is worth checking before you upload anything, not after you've relied on it for years.

These risks are real, not paranoia, and they don't disappear because a provider is polished or well-marketed. The point of the next section is to turn "is this safe" into something you can actually check, for any service — including ours.

## What "safely online" should actually mean

Treat this as a checklist you can hold any provider to, not just Vaulto:

1. **Encryption at rest.** Records and uploaded files should be encrypted when they're stored, with a specific, named mechanism behind it — not just the word "encryption" used as a general reassurance.
2. **Isolation.** Your account should be kept separate from other users' data by design, not by a promise.
3. **No staff browsing.** Support and staff should not be able to browse into your data as a matter of course. If a company can't say plainly who can see your files and under what circumstances, that vagueness is your answer.
4. **Exportability.** You should be able to get everything out — the records and the original files, not just a summary — at any time, in a form you can use elsewhere.
5. **No false absolutes.** Be wary of an unqualified privacy promise — the suggestion that the company itself is somehow shut out from ever accessing your data — sitting next to features like search, AI-assisted entry, or customer support that can look something up for you. Those features require the company to be able to read your data to work, so the sweeping promise and the data-reading feature are usually in tension, and a service claiming both hasn't been precise about either.

Any provider that answers all five specifically has told you something useful. One that answers with a superlative and stops there hasn't — a strong-sounding adjective isn't a mechanism.

## Best of both: the original where it must stay, a digital copy everywhere else

The two approaches aren't actually competing — used together, they cover each other's weak points.

- **Keep legally-required originals physical.** Wills, some certificates, and property deeds often need to exist on paper to be valid. Keep those specific documents in one protected physical place — a fireproof safe or a bank box — and nowhere else.
- **Keep a digital copy of everything.** Scan the originals and add everything else — statements, policies, ID documents, valuations — to an organised, access-controlled digital record where your records and uploaded files are encrypted in transit and at rest. This is what solves "where did we put it": one indexed place, reachable when it's actually needed.
- **Export periodically, as your own backup.** Because a digital vault's export is a copy you hold — a file you take with you, the same way a printed statement is yours the moment it leaves the printer — treat an occasional export as your own independent backup — belt and braces, not a sign you don't trust the service.

That combination — a small set of protected originals, a complete organised digital record, and a copy you hold yourself — covers fire and flood, a company changing terms, and the family member who just needs to find the right document at the worst possible time.
