Most people set up a power of attorney and then file it away, relieved it's done. But the document is only half the job. A financial power of attorney gives someone the legal authority to act for you — it doesn't give them the faintest idea of what you own, where it is, or what needs paying each month. If the day ever comes when they have to step in, that missing half is the difference between a smooth handover and a stressful scavenger hunt through drawers, inboxes, and old statements.

This is a practical guide to the second half: **what to organise so an attorney can actually manage your finances**. It's built to be worked through category by category, kept in one place, and handed over cleanly when it's needed.

> This is general information to help you get organised, not legal advice. The rules for power of attorney differ between England and Wales, Scotland, and Northern Ireland — and between countries — and they change over time. Confirm the specifics with a qualified solicitor or your local Office of the Public Guardian before you act.

## What a financial power of attorney actually covers

A financial power of attorney lets a person you choose — your **attorney** — make money decisions on your behalf if you're unable to. In England and Wales this is a **property and financial affairs Lasting Power of Attorney (LPA)**, which has to be **registered with the Office of the Public Guardian** before anyone can use it. Scotland has a **Continuing Power of Attorney** for financial matters, and Northern Ireland still uses the older **Enduring Power of Attorney**. Whichever applies to you, the core duty is the same: an attorney must act in your best interests, keep your money separate from theirs, and keep good records.

Notice what the document does *not* do. It grants authority, but it doesn't hand over a picture of your finances. Your attorney arrives with the legal right to act — and, very often, no idea which bank holds your current account, whether there's a second pension, or when the buildings insurance renews. Organising that picture in advance is the single most useful thing you can do to make the authority you've granted actually workable.

## Why organising the finances matters more than the paperwork

The paperwork — setting up and registering the LPA — is the part everyone focuses on, because it's the part with forms and a solicitor. But by the time an attorney needs to act, the forms are done. What they're left holding is a question: *what is there, and where?*

Without an answer, the job becomes detective work at the worst possible time. Accounts get missed. A policy lapses because no one knew it existed. Bills go unpaid on an account no one could find. None of that is a failure of authority — it's a failure of information. A short, current record of what you own and where the proof lives turns weeks of hunting into an afternoon of orientation. ## The financial checklist to organise

Work through these categories and, for each item, note the two things that matter: **what it is** and **where to find it**. You won't have every line — skip what doesn't apply.

### Where your money sits

- Current accounts (bank and building society)
- Savings and cash ISAs
- Premium Bonds and National Savings
- Any joint accounts, and who else is on them

List the provider and roughly where the details live — **not** the PINs or online banking passwords. An attorney deals with accounts through the proper channels once the LPA is registered; they don't need your credentials, and sharing them can breach your bank's terms. (More on why in [should you put passwords in your will](/blog/should-you-put-passwords-in-your-will/).)

### What comes in

- State Pension and any private or workplace pensions
- Salary or self-employment income
- Benefits and allowances
- Rental or investment income

### What goes out

- Mortgage or rent
- Loans, credit cards, and finance agreements
- Direct debits and standing orders (utilities, council tax, subscriptions)
- Care costs, if any

### What you own

- Your home and any other property, with the deeds' location
- Vehicles
- Valuables — jewellery, art, antiques, collections
- Business interests

### Investments and savings

- Stocks, shares, and investment ISAs
- Workplace and personal pensions (with provider details)
- Any trusts you're involved in

### What you owe

An honest picture includes the liabilities, not just the assets — the mortgage balance, outstanding loans, and card balances. An attorney managing your money needs to see both sides.

### Insurance

- Life insurance
- Home, buildings, and contents
- Health and any income-protection cover
- Vehicle insurance

### The essential documents

- The **registered LPA itself** — the single most important document, and the one banks will ask to see
- Property deeds and title documents
- Your will
- Pension and insurance policy documents
- Recent statements and your latest tax paperwork

Keep a note of where each original lives. For a fuller version of this list, see [the important documents every family should keep](/blog/important-family-documents-to-keep/) and [where to keep important financial documents](/blog/where-to-keep-important-financial-documents/).

### Digital and online accounts

More of your financial life is online than on paper — accounts with no statements in the post, e-savings providers, digital-only banks. Note that these exist and how they're reached, so nothing invisible slips through the gaps.

### The key people

Write down who to call: your solicitor, financial adviser, accountant, and the main contact at your bank. When an attorney knows who already helps with your money, half their questions answer themselves.

## Keep it current

A record like this goes stale the moment you finish it — a new account, a switched provider, a moved file. The fix is a small rhythm, not more effort:

- **Update as you go.** When something changes, change the record then, not "later."
- **Review once a year.** A short annual pass keeps it trustworthy.
- **Tell someone it exists.** The most organised record in the world helps no one if your attorney doesn't know it's there. Make sure the person you've named — and ideally your solicitor — knows where to find it.

Setting up the power of attorney protects who *can* act. Organising your finances protects how *well* they can. Do both, and you've turned a legal formality into something your family can actually use.
