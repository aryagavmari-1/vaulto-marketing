If something happened to you this week — a bad fall, a hospital stay, worse — could the people closest to you find what they'd need? Not eventually, after weeks of calling banks and searching drawers, but that day: the life insurance policy, which bank actually holds your savings, where the will is, who the solicitor is.

For most families the honest answer is "not easily," and it's rarely because nobody's organised. It's because the organising exists in one person's head, in one login, in one drawer nobody else has opened. A **family emergency binder** is the fix — a single, findable place that turns what you know into something your family could use without you standing next to them.

This is written UK-first, but the shape of it travels; specifics on wills, probate, and access after death vary by country, so treat the categories as a starting point and confirm anything legal against your own situation.

## What a family emergency binder is — and why paper alone falls short

An emergency binder isn't a legal document and it isn't a will. It's a plain answer to one question: if something happened to you tomorrow, would your family know what you have, where it is, and who to call? Some households keep this as an actual ring binder; others as a folder of scanned documents; the format matters less than whether it's complete and findable. If you want the stripped-down version for the acute moment — the few things someone needs in the first hours of an emergency, before the full record matters — our [in-case-of-emergency (ICE) file guide](/blog/in-case-of-emergency-ice-file/) is the companion piece to this one.

The traditional version — a physical folder in a filing cabinet — gets people most of the way there and then quietly fails in three places. It's a single copy, so if it's lost, damaged, or the one person who knows where it lives is the person who's unavailable, it might as well not exist. It goes stale the moment an account closes or a policy changes, because updating a printed page means reprinting it, so most binders get built once and never touched again. And it can't hold anything digital — an emailed policy document or a photo of a serial number either gets printed and filed or, more often, just doesn't make it in.

None of that means paper is wrong. It means a binder built only on paper is carrying three risks its owner usually hasn't priced in.

## The checklist: what actually goes in it

Seven categories cover almost everything a family needs in an emergency. Don't aim for a perfect list on the first pass — a rough, organised start beats a complete one that never gets finished.

**1. Key people.** Whoever your family would need to call, with a name and a number against each: solicitor, accountant, financial adviser, executor, GP. Our [executor checklist](/blog/executor-checklist/) goes deeper on what the named executor specifically will need once things move to probate.

**2. Accounts and assets.** A plain list of what you have and who holds it — current and savings accounts, pensions (including old workplace ones people forget), ISAs and investments, and any debts like a mortgage, loan, or card. Name the provider and roughly what it is; exact balances aren't the point.

**3. Property.** What you own and where the paperwork sits — the home itself, a second property, a storage unit, a safe-deposit box. Our guide to [making a home inventory](/blog/how-to-make-a-home-inventory/) covers the room-by-room version of this if you also want a record for insurance purposes.

**4. Insurance.** Life, critical illness, home, and anything else that pays out or matters in a crisis — these are the policies families most often miss, because nobody looks at them month to month. A provider and policy type against each is usually enough.

**5. Important documents, and exactly where each one lives.** The will and who holds the signed copy, property deeds, insurance paperwork, ID documents, tax records. What matters here is location, not the document itself — our [checklist of important family documents to keep](/blog/important-family-documents-to-keep/) breaks down the fuller list and how long to keep each one.

**6. Medical information.** Allergies, ongoing conditions, current medications, GP surgery details — the kind of thing that matters most in exactly the moment nobody can remember it clearly.

**7. Digital access.** Not passwords written in plain text, which creates its own risk, but a note of which email account resets everything else, which accounts exist, and where the household actually keeps that information safely.

## Paper vs. digital: the honest trade-off

Most people default to one of two approaches, and it's worth being clear-eyed about what each one actually costs.

A **paper folder** is simple to start and needs no login — you can begin this evening with a pen. Its weaknesses are the ones above: one copy, goes stale quietly, and can't hold digital material without printing it.

A **shared digital record** solves findability and freshness — reachable from anywhere, updated in minutes — but only if it's actually private. A spreadsheet on a shared drive or a note synced to every phone in the house isn't safer than the folder; it's just a different way of leaving the same information exposed, especially if it holds anything sensitive in plain text.

The practical middle ground is a private, organised digital record you control, where each item sits next to the paperwork that belongs to it, and where updating an entry takes a minute rather than a reprint. That's the gap Vaulto is built to fill — and printed or exported when your family needs a physical copy in hand, so you're not choosing one format at the expense of the other.

## Build it fast

The reason most emergency binders never get finished isn't lack of intent — it's that "list everything we own" is a big, vague task with no obvious starting point, so it gets put off.

Vaulto is built around short, guided **capture sprints** rather than one long sitting: a room sprint, a category sprint, a documents sprint, a 20-things sprint for a fast first pass across whatever matters most, and a partner session for building it with someone else. Pick whichever matches the ten minutes you actually have, and the binder grows in short bursts instead of waiting for a weekend that doesn't come.

Each entry can carry more than a name — where it's kept, its insurance status, and any notes or tags you want to attach — and the paperwork you photograph or upload sits alongside the entry it belongs to, in the same place you'd look for it. That's what turns the checklist above from a list of bullet points into an actual, usable record.

For anything with paperwork — a policy schedule, a statement, a certificate — you can photograph it and Vaulto will suggest the fields for you. That speeds up the typing considerably; it doesn't skip you. Every suggestion lands in a review queue first, and nothing is saved to your binder until you've looked at it and confirmed it's right.

A **partner session** lets you invite someone specific in to build or check entries alongside you — useful for splitting the work, or for having a second person confirm the list is accurate before you consider it done. It's scoped to that session, not a standing shared login to your whole account.

Once the first pass exists, treat it as a five-minute habit rather than a project: update an entry when something changes — a switched provider, a closed account, a new adviser — and it stays accurate for less effort than the original binder took to build.

## Keep a copy your family can hold

A digital binder is only useful in an emergency if the right person can actually get to it — which is worth being precise about, because it's the part most emergency-binder advice glosses over.

When it's time for your family or your solicitor to have a copy, you can export everything you've recorded, including your files, as a set they can use directly — the digital equivalent of handing over the folder, minus the trip to find it. It's a copy you hand over on your own timing, not a live account someone else logs into, and not a switch that unlocks automatically on a given date. If your plan depends on a specific person being able to reach this the moment they need it, that's worth deciding — and acting on — deliberately, rather than assuming it happens on its own.

## Frequently asked questions

**What should be in a family emergency binder?**
Seven categories cover it: key people and contacts, accounts and assets, property, insurance, important documents, medical information, and digital access. For each item, the useful details are usually just the provider or institution, roughly what it is, and where the paperwork or record lives — not exhaustive detail. A binder that lists people to call but not what to tell them, or accounts but not where the paperwork is, is only half-built.

**Is a physical binder or a digital one better?**
