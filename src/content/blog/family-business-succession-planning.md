Family business succession usually stalls in the same place, and it isn't the hard legal question. It's the basic one: nobody has actually written down what the business consists of — who owns what, what it owns, what it owes, and where the paperwork lives. Without that, a solicitor or accountant can't structure anything, because there's nothing solid to structure.

This guide is the readiness half of succession: a category-by-category checklist for mapping the business before you sit down with a professional, plus how to get family or co-directors helping without handing over control.

It's written UK-first — rules, and reliefs like business property relief, vary by country — but the categories and the method travel.

## Succession has two halves, and only one is yours to do

**The structuring** — how ownership actually transfers, what a shareholders' agreement should say, how any inheritance tax reliefs apply, what a fair split looks like across children with different levels of involvement — is professional work. A solicitor and an accountant do that, together, once they know what they're structuring.

**The inventory** comes first, and it's the part that's usually missing. It's a complete, accurate picture of what the business is: the shares, the property it runs from, the equipment, the intellectual property, the money owed in both directions, the contracts that keep it running, and the insurance covering it. Most family businesses have this information — it's just scattered across a filing cabinet, an accountant's old emails, and one person's memory.

Building that picture is what this article covers. It's the thing you can do before the first meeting, and it's usually what makes that meeting productive instead of an admin exercise.

## What a family business succession plan checklist actually covers

Work through categories rather than trusting memory — that's what catches the things people assume are "obvious" and never write down.

- **Ownership and shareholdings.** Who owns what percentage, and how it's structured — a limited company, a partnership, a sole trader arrangement with informal family involvement. Vaulto has categories built for exactly this: **Business interest**, **Partnership interest**, **Shareholder loan**, **Intellectual property**, and **Other financial interest**, sitting alongside the rest of what you own.
- **Property and premises.** The building or land the business operates from, whether it's owned outright, mortgaged, or leased — filed as **Commercial property** or **Land**.
- **Plant, equipment and vehicles.** Machinery, tools, fleet vehicles, fixtures — the physical things that let the business run day to day.
- **Intellectual property.** Trademarks, patents, registered designs, a domain name, a brand — anything that has value independent of who's running the business.
- **Money the business owes you.** A director's loan or shareholder loan you've put in over the years is real personal money tied up in the business, and it's easy to lose track of the running total.
- **What the business owes.** Loans, any guarantee you've personally signed on the business's behalf, and tax due — three separate things that all change what the business is actually worth once you net them off.
- **Key contracts.** The lease, a major supplier or customer agreement, a franchise or licence — the documents that would matter to whoever takes over, stored as records with the paperwork attached.
- **Insurance.** Key-person cover, business property and contents cover, professional indemnity — whatever's in place to protect the business itself, not just its owners.

Two things are worth naming honestly, because a business succession checklist can quietly imply more precision than it has. **Goodwill** — the value of reputation, customer relationships and trading history — doesn't have its own category; it's not something you can photograph or file a document for in the way a lease or a loan statement works, so it belongs with a valuation from an accountant, not a line in an inventory app. Likewise, **plant and machinery** and **key contracts** without a category of their own get filed under a general **Other asset** or **Important document** entry — real records, just not a bespoke schema. Naming the categories that exist is more useful than implying ones that don't.

If a category is genuinely empty for your business, note that and move on — the value of the checklist is confirming you considered it, not filling every line.

## How to build the picture, step by step

1. **Set your columns before you start.** Item, category, rough value or a note that it's still to be confirmed, who legally owns it, where the proof lives. Capturing into structure from the first entry saves a re-sort later.
2. **Do a paperwork pass first.** Shareholder agreements, the lease, loan statements, insurance schedules, the accountant's most recent set of accounts. Photograph each document as you go — Vaulto turns a photo into a draft record you then review and confirm, which is faster than typing everything out by hand, and the original stays attached as evidence.
3. **Do a walk-through of the premises.** Equipment, vehicles, fixtures — anything physical that a room-by-room pass catches and a memory-only list misses.
4. **Add what's owed in both directions.** Director and shareholder loans in, business debt, guarantees and tax due out. Both sides change what the business is genuinely worth.
5. **Leave gaps rather than skipping entries.** If you can't value something yet — especially goodwill, which usually needs a proper valuation — list it and mark it to confirm. A flagged gap is a complete list; a missing item isn't.

## Passing it to your children — what changes, and what doesn't

An even split on paper rarely means a fair outcome in practice. A child who's worked in the business for fifteen years and one who's never set foot in it aren't in the same position, even if a valuation says their shares are worth the same. Getting to something that's actually fair — rather than just arithmetically equal — starts from an accurate picture of what there is to divide: real ownership percentages, real debts, real contracts, not a rough sense of "the business is probably worth about X."

That's also where the practical risks in a handover tend to surface once the inventory is honest: a single person who holds all the supplier relationships and institutional knowledge, shareholdings that were never formally documented when the business started, or a chosen successor who isn't yet across the parts of the business that matter. None of that gets solved by software — it's exactly the conversation to have with a solicitor and accountant — but it's a much shorter conversation once the ownership, debts and contracts are already written down in one place.
