Working out what an estate is worth is one of the first practical jobs of dealing with probate — and one of the most fiddly. It isn't one number you look up; it's a list you build, category by category, using the right kind of value for each one, with evidence to back it up if HMRC or a beneficiary ever asks.

This is a plain-English walkthrough of how estate valuation for probate actually works in the UK: what "value" means in this context, how to work through each category of asset, when you genuinely need a professional, and how to bring it all together into a total you can stand behind.

> *This is general information to help you understand the process, not legal, tax, or financial advice. Probate and inheritance tax valuation rules are detailed and change over time — confirm current requirements on GOV.UK, in the HMRC IHT400 guidance, or with a solicitor or accountant before you rely on any figure.*

## What "value for probate" actually means

Probate valuation isn't about what something cost, what it might be worth next year, or its insurance replacement value. HMRC wants the **open market value** — broadly, what a willing buyer would have paid a willing seller for that asset on the open market — as at one specific date: the **date of death**. This is why you'll see the term "date-of-death valuation" throughout probate paperwork. A share price from a month before, or a house valuation from a year after, isn't the figure that belongs on the form.

It's also worth knowing the difference between two totals you'll come across:

- **Gross estate** — everything owned, added up, with no deductions. Every account, property, investment and possession goes in at its date-of-death value.
- **Net estate** — the gross estate minus what's owed: mortgages, loans, credit cards, unpaid bills, and reasonable funeral costs. Inheritance tax, if any is due, is calculated on the net figure, not the gross one.

For most estates, this total gets declared either through the online probate application (simpler estates) or the fuller HMRC IHT400 return (larger or more complex ones). Either way, the underlying task is identical: list what's owned and owed, value each item on the right date, and be able to show your working.

## Category by category: valuing what's in the estate

Different asset types are valued differently. Working through them one at a time is more manageable than trying to size up "the estate" in one go.

**Property.** The family home and any other land or property is usually the single largest — and most scrutinised — item. A written valuation from a local estate agent is often accepted for straightforward, lower-value estates, but a formal RICS "red book" valuation from a chartered surveyor is the safer route for higher-value property, unusual properties, or where beneficiaries might disagree on the figure.

**Bank and savings accounts.** Ask each bank or building society for a balance "as at" the date of death — most have a standard process for this once notified. Include current accounts, savings accounts, and cash ISAs.

**Investments and pensions.** Shares, funds, and other investments are valued using the quoted price on the date of death (or a standard averaging method HMRC accepts for that date). Pensions are their own case: many workplace and personal pensions with death benefits paid at the pension provider's discretion sit outside the estate for inheritance tax purposes, while others don't — this is worth checking per policy rather than assuming.

**Possessions.** Furniture, cars, jewellery, and general household contents are valued at what they'd realistically fetch second-hand — not the original purchase price, and not an insurance replacement figure, which usually runs much higher. Individual items worth more than roughly £1,500 — a piece of jewellery, art, an antique, a collection — are generally valued separately by a specialist rather than folded into a rough "contents" estimate.

**Life insurance policies.** If a policy pays out on death and isn't written in trust, the payout typically forms part of the estate and is valued at the amount paid out. A policy written in trust usually passes outside the estate.

**Liabilities and debts.** Mortgages, loans, credit card balances, and unpaid bills are listed and subtracted, along with funeral costs, to get from the gross to the net estate.

## When you need a professional valuation

You don't need a specialist for everything — a bank statement or a quoted share price is already a defensible figure. Bring in a professional for:

- **Property**, especially anything high-value, unusual, jointly owned, or where beneficiaries might not agree on the number.
- **Individual possessions over roughly £1,500** — jewellery, art, antiques, collections — where a rough guess is likely to be wrong in either direction.

HMRC can query a valuation it considers too low, and an estate that's undervalued (even by accident) can end up owing more tax and interest later, or facing delays while a figure is disputed. A professional valuation for the items that matter gives the estate something to point to if it's ever questioned.

## Frequently asked questions

### Who values assets for probate?

The executor or administrator is responsible for arriving at a figure for everything the estate owns and owes, usually with professional help for the categories that need it — a surveyor or estate agent for property, a specialist for high-value possessions, and the deceased's own banks, pension providers, and insurers for account and policy balances.

### Do you need a professional valuer for probate?

Not for every item. Bank balances and quoted shares can usually be valued directly from statements. Property and individual possessions worth more than roughly £1,500 are the categories where a professional valuation is expected, partly because HMRC can query a figure it considers too low.

### What is a date-of-death valuation?

It's the value of an asset specifically on the day the person died — the open market value on that date — rather than what it cost, what it's worth now, or what it later sells for.

### What's the difference between the gross and net estate?

The gross estate is everything owned, added together with no deductions. The net estate subtracts what's owed — debts, loans, and reasonable funeral costs — and it's the net figure that inheritance tax is calculated on.

### Do small estates still need a full valuation?

Broadly yes — you still need to work out roughly what everything is worth to confirm an estate qualifies as small or exempt. What usually differs is the paperwork: simpler estates can often use the online probate service without a full IHT return, while larger or more complex ones need the fuller HMRC process.

### Are possessions valued at what they cost or what they'd sell for?

At realistic resale value, not the original price and not an insurance replacement figure. Everyday contents are usually worth surprisingly little on this basis; individually valuable items like jewellery or art are the exception and are valued separately.
