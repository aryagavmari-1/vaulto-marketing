Divorce is stressful enough without a paperwork scramble. Yet almost every financial settlement starts in the same place: both people have to say, clearly and completely, what they own and what they owe. Lawyers call it **financial disclosure**, and getting it right — or wrong — shapes everything that follows.

This guide is the calm, practical version of that first step: how to build a complete list of your assets and debts for divorce disclosure, worked through category by category so nothing gets missed. It's written UK-first, but the method travels — the categories and the "list everything, value it roughly, keep the proof" approach apply wherever you are, even where the specific forms and law differ.

One thing up front, because it matters: this is a guide to organising **your own** finances honestly, not a way to investigate anyone else's. More on that below.

## Why the list comes first

In most divorces, both parties owe a duty of **full and frank disclosure** — you're expected to lay out everything you own and owe, not just the obvious things. In England and Wales that's usually captured on **Form E**; Scotland, and each US state, use their own financial statements. The form differs; the substance doesn't. Before any negotiation about who gets what, the court and the solicitors need a complete, honest picture of the pot.

Building your list early pays off three ways:

- **It speeds everything up.** Disclosure is the slowest, most back-and-forth stage of many divorces. Arriving with a complete list — values and documents attached — removes weeks of chasing.
- **It lowers the legal bill.** Solicitors charge for time. Handing over an organised picture rather than a carrier bag of statements means they spend billable hours on advice, not admin.
- **It protects you.** Disclosure you can stand behind — sole accounts, an old pension, an overseas asset, all of it — keeps the process clean. Assets that surface later can reopen a settlement and damage your credibility.

## What you have to disclose: the complete checklist

Completeness is the whole point, so work through categories rather than memory. These buckets, between them, catch almost everything a person owns:

- **Property and land** — the family home, plus any second home, buy-to-let, holiday place, or share in a plot. Include property held in one name only.
- **Bank and savings accounts** — every current account, savings account, ISA or tax-advantaged account, and cash held elsewhere. Sole accounts count too.
- **Pensions** — often the second-largest asset after the home, and the most commonly under-listed. Include workplace schemes (current and old), personal pensions, and any state pension entitlement. Check payslips and old employment records for schemes you've lost track of.
- **Investments and shares** — stocks, funds, bonds, shares in a private company, crypto, and share-save or option schemes from an employer.
- **Business interests** — any company you own or co-own, self-employment, or a partnership. These usually need a professional valuation; your job now is to list them.
- **Vehicles** — cars, motorbikes, a caravan, a boat — anything with a logbook or title.
- **Valuables and contents** — jewellery, watches, art, collections, high-end tech. A room-by-room walk catches the ones you'd never recall from a chair.
- **Money owed to you and other assets** — loans you've made, a director's loan, a tax rebate due, an inheritance received or expected.
- **What you owe** — the mortgage, car finance, credit cards, personal and student loans, and any tax owed. Debts are part of disclosure too, and they change the real shape of the settlement.

If a category is empty for you, tick it off and move on — the value of the checklist is confirming you *considered* it.

## How to build the list, step by step

The reason lists fail isn't difficulty, it's gaps. Two passes fix that, because each one surfaces what the other misses.

**1. Set up your columns first.** Decide where the list lives before you start, so you capture into structure. Five columns do the job: *item, category, rough value, whose name it's in, where the proof lives.*

**2. Do an account-by-account pass.** Open your banking app, your email, and your document drawer, and log every provider you see — accounts, pensions, investments, insurance, subscriptions. This sweep catches the *invisible* assets: the dormant savings account, the old workplace pension, the policy set up years ago.

**3. Do a room-by-room pass.** Walk the house and log the physical, valuable, and proof-bearing things — electronics, jewellery, tools, the art on the wall, the documents in the filing cabinet. Photograph as you go; a photo is faster than a sentence and doubles as evidence.

**4. Attach the proof.** Disclosure isn't just a number — it's a number you can back up. Statements, valuations, deeds, pension statements, and payslips are what make the list credible. Keep each with the item it proves.

**5. Add what you owe, then leave gaps rather than skipping.** Finish with debts so the list reflects your real position. If you're unsure of a value, enter the item, mark it "to confirm," and move on — a gap still counts; a forgotten item doesn't.

Doing both passes in one sitting is what makes the list *complete*: the account sweep and the physical walk rarely turn up the same things. ## About the *other* side's assets — the honest part

If you searched for an "asset list for divorce," you may be worried your spouse won't disclose everything. That's a real and common concern — but it's important to be clear about how it actually gets handled, because no app or spreadsheet solves it.

Uncovering assets a spouse hasn't disclosed is **legal work, not software**. It happens through the court's disclosure process, formal questionnaires, and — where there's genuine suspicion — a **forensic accountant** instructed by your solicitor, who can examine records you can't access yourself. No consumer app can log into or "discover" another person's accounts, and you shouldn't trust one that claims it can.

What you *can* control is your own side. The most useful thing you can do is make your disclosure so complete and well-documented that the process stays clean and fast — and note, factually and without accusation, anything you genuinely know exists but can't value, so your solicitor can pursue it properly. Keep it accurate; keep it calm.

## A note on doing this well

Two habits make disclosure smoother than almost anything else: **disclose fully** — including sole accounts, old pensions, and overseas assets, and let your solicitor decide what's relevant rather than deciding for them — and **keep the proof with the number**, so nothing you claim can be waved away. Get those two right and you've done the hard part of the financial side.

## Frequently asked questions

**What assets do you have to disclose in a divorce?**
In most divorces you're expected to give full and frank disclosure of everything you own and owe, not just the family home — property and land, all bank and savings accounts, pensions, investments and shares, business interests, vehicles, valuables, and any debts, including assets held only in your name or overseas. The exact form depends on where you divorce (in England and Wales it's usually Form E; other countries and US states use their own financial statements), but the duty to disclose fully is near-universal. A solicitor confirms what applies to you; your job first is to make sure nothing is left off the list.

**How do I make a list of assets for a divorce?**
Work through categories rather than trusting memory. Do two passes: an account-by-account sweep of your banking app, email and paperwork, then a room-by-room walk of the house for physical valuables and documents. For each item note a category, a rough value, and where the proof lives. Add what you owe. Photograph statements and valuables as you go. Leave a value blank rather than skipping an item; a gap still counts, a missing entry doesn't.

**Can an app find my spouse's hidden assets?**
No. No consumer app can log into or aggregate another person's accounts without their credentials, and none can "discover" someone else's unknown accounts for you. Uncovering undisclosed assets is legal work — done through the court's disclosure process and, where needed, a forensic accountant instructed by your solicitor.
